## Judgment calls

1. "New York has already shown that writing rules into systems works." Changed to "New York has shown that a rule written into a data system can close at least one channel." I also added the study's limits, from C22: "The study also found that ICE and Customs and Border Protection traffic stops are common across New York, and it could not test whether the data broker route was closed."

2. "with results reported in the audits Executive Order 13 already requires." Removed. The third contract term now ends "with regular reports to the mayor on what they show." The proposal has a new closing paragraph that describes the order accurately: "Executive Order 13 required reports to the mayor only once, by May 7, and only from six agencies about their own policies on immigration authorities. None of it covers vendors. A follow-up order could require those vendor reports on a schedule, starting with the contracts behind the NYPD's five years of plate data." The six agencies are ACS, DOC, DOHMH, Probation, DSS and NYPD (C7). The essay doesn't say whether the May 7 reports were published, since nobody could confirm it.

3. Flock's retention and filtering claims. Both are now attributed: "Flock says it has cut its own default to seven days, from 30" and "a filter Flock says it now offers." The old text said "Flock itself now defaults." The chart dek now reads "Flock figures are defaults as the company announced them." I left the row label "Flock default since Aug. 2026" alone because it is chart data.

4. Chief privacy officer designation. I left "A camera vendor falls outside that definition unless the chief privacy officer designates it." It's conditional and accurate, and it doesn't claim that no designation exists.

5. The 2022 amendment to the identifying information law. No change. The quoted definitions are from the 2017 text. See the decision for Josh below.

6. Fairness. The objection section still concedes that fingerprints, brokers and benefit and tax records are out of the City's reach. It now also carries the UW study's own limits, so the reply to the objection is weaker than before and more honest.

## For Josh to decide

- The essay still says "A camera vendor falls outside that definition unless the chief privacy officer designates it." Nobody could confirm whether any such designation exists. If one does, the "start with a designation" proposal has partly happened already. A call to the Office of Information Privacy would settle it.
- The identifying information law is quoted from its 2017 text. It was amended in 2022, and nobody compared the current Administrative Code wording. The "contractor" definition carries the proposal, so it should be checked against the codified text before publication.
- The "since at least 2022" Palantir phrasing understates. The dossier notes earlier awards back to 2019 that nobody verified. I left it as is.
- The new closing paragraph proposes a follow-up executive order. That is a recommendation, not a fact. Josh may prefer to end on the three contract terms instead and drop it.
- The chart title changed to "Actual and proposed limits on keeping plate data range from two days to five years," because the two-day figure is a stalled bill, not law. The old title, "varies by a factor of almost 1,000," was arithmetically correct (1,825 / 2 ≈ 912) but mixed the bill in with laws and policies.
