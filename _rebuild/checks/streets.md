## Errors found and fixed
- "A Hunter College team found the opposite for New York, then retracted the study after Waymo identified flaws; the revised version found Waymo had 62 percent fewer injury crashes than the city's for-hire vehicles." → "A Hunter College team comparing Waymo's record elsewhere with New York's for-hire drivers first reported a higher rate of serious-injury crashes, then retracted that analysis after Waymo pointed out flaws; the revised version found Waymo had 62 percent fewer injury crashes per mile, in the cities where it operates, than the city's for-hire vehicles, and called that 'not yet a conclusion for New York City.'" Why: the study never measured Waymo in New York (Waymo has no driverless miles here); it compared Waymo's national data with NYC for-hire vehicles. The original found fewer injury crashes overall but a higher serious-injury rate, so "the opposite" overstated it. Only the safety analysis was retracted (July 29, 2026), not the whole report. The 62 percent figure is per mile, Waymo national data, and the revised report itself says it is "not yet a conclusion for New York City." ## Judgment calls for the editor
- Super Speeder: "under which repeat offenders can be required to install speed limiters." The state law requires a City local law first, takes effect one year after enactment (about May 2027), applies only to school-zone speed-camera liabilities, and requires the devices to allow a manual override. "Speed limiters" is the sponsor's own term, but the City's Sept. 15 release does not itself say the program will use them. The essay's link goes to the City release, not the statute.
- Waymo: the state renewed its testing authority to 2028 in May, so the lapse in New York City is now a City decision. The essay's "has not renewed them" is accurate. The editor may want to say the state authority exists, since that sharpens the point. I did not add it.
- Red-light cameras: "as of January, cameras were still at just 150" is accurate as dated, but stale. March reporting describes DOT activating about 50 new sites a week. I found no October count. Consider "DOT began adding cameras in January and said it would reach 600 by the end of this year."
- The NY1 figures and the DOT 94 percent and 14 percent figures are agency self-reports. The essay flags both, which is fair.
- "Of all the automation arriving on New York City's streets, cameras ... have the strongest evidence behind them" and the dek's "best record" are opinion, framed as the author's argument. The case-against section is fair, but it does not mention the volume of rejected events (44.6 percent). A critic could read that number as evidence of noisy detection; the essay reads it as the human and rule filter working.
- "Whether a speed camera reduces crashes on the block where it sits has been answered." The PNAS study measures effects near camera intersections over seven months, so this is a fair summary. "Answered" is strong but defensible given two independent studies.
