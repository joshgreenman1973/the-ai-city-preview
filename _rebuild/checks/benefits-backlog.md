## Errors found and fixed
- "The law gives a two-year reprieve to states whose rate, multiplied by 1.5, reaches 20 percent." → "The law gives a one-year reprieve, to fiscal 2029, to states whose 2025 rate, multiplied by 1.5, reaches 20 percent. ... (A state whose fiscal 2026 rate clears the same bar gets two years.)" Why: the cost share starts in FY2028; the FY2025 clause moves it to FY2029 (one year); only the FY2026 clause moves it to FY2030. P.L. 119-21 sec. 10105: "If, for fiscal year 2025, the payment error rate of a State multiplied by 1.5 is equal to or above 20 percent, the implementation date ... shall be fiscal year 2029." https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm ; CRS IN12723 agrees ("delayed to FY2029"), https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-08-27_IN12723_aec1f176f4ca6f1aad2e7252247d9f1a13e9d181.html. (The writer likely took "two-year" from Hunger Solutions New York, which uses it loosely.) The original also implied New York is out of reach of any delay; its FY2026 rate (published around June 2027) could still qualify it, hence the parenthetical.
- "the City attributes the decline largely to the law" → "the City ties the decline to a national drop driven by the law." Why: the MMR says the city's changes "are aligned with nationwide trends driven by the effects of" H.R. 1; it does not say the city's own decline is largely caused by the law. https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/hra.pdf
- "The Mayor's Management Report credits some of the cash assistance gain to 'a new technology tool...'" → "In explaining the cash assistance gain, the Mayor's Management Report says that to improve timeliness HRA implemented 'a new technology tool...'" Why: the MMR says the tool was implemented "to improve timeliness"; it does not attribute any measured share of the gain to it. Same URL.
- Broken link markup "[July 4, 2025],(https://...)" → "[July 4, 2025](https://...)," so the link renders.
- ABAWD age: "in the City's description, raised the age covered by the rules from 54 to 64" (linked to the MMR) → "in the Agriculture Department's implementing guidance, raised the age covered by the rules from 54 to 64" (linked to the USDA memo). Resolution of the "over 65" question: the statute (sec. 10102(a)) rewrites the exception to "(A) under 18, or over 65, years of age." USDA's Oct. 3, 2025 implementation memo reads this as: "The OBBB increases the age of those subject to the time limit to age 64. Therefore, individuals aged 18 to 64 are now subject to the time limit," up from 18 to 54. So "54 to 64" is the official federal reading, not just the City's; a literal reading of "over 65" would cover age 65 too, but USDA's guidance governs how states apply it. https://www.usda.gov/sites/default/files/guidance-documents/fns.snap-obbb-abawd-exceptions-implementation.pdf (403 to direct fetch; copy pulled from the Wayback Machine). The parent exemption narrowed to a child under 14 is confirmed by both the law and the memo.

## Judgment calls for the editor
- Erin Dalton (note only; not in the essay): she is NYC's Department of Social Services commissioner, appointed by Mayor Mamdani Feb. 25, 2026 (the MMR says "appointed ... in February 2026"; the settlement footnote gives an effective date of March 2, 2026). She previously directed the Allegheny County Department of Human Services (18 years there, director from 2021), the agency behind the Allegheny Family Screening Tool. If the essay names her as the official who "could adopt" the rule, that background is relevant and could cut either way.
- Forest settlement status: the essay's "still before" the judge is supported, but a July 14 "Fairness Hearing" entry and September extension orders suggest something is pending (possibly supplemental filings). If someone can pull the PACER docket before publication, do it. The opening phrase "A class action that ends partly in a pop-up window" is fine as framing, but the settlement is not final yet.
- Arkansas: The Verge says the diabetes coding problem "came out in the court case" and lists the cerebral palsy error as "a separate problem" in the same passage. "Litigation later turned up" the cerebral palsy error is a fair reading, but slightly firmer than the article.
- Fair hearings: the 13.6 percent upheld rate covers all HRA hearings with decisions (cash assistance, SNAP, Medicaid and others), not SNAP alone. The essay says "HRA clients," which is accurate, but a reader could take it as SNAP-only.
- "The record of the past dozen years": MiDAS (2013) to now is 13 years. Fine as rough phrasing.
- The 54-to-64 age change: the statute's literal text ("over 65") and USDA's reading (subject through 64) differ by a year. The essay now follows USDA, which is how states apply it. If an editor wants the law itself cited, say "raised the upper age for the time limit to the mid-60s."
- Hunger Solutions' "$1.2 billion a year" is an advocacy estimate, labeled as such. Its own description of a "two-year delay" is wrong for the FY2025 clause, so don't lean on that group for the mechanics.
- Fairness: the "case for automating the no" section states the error-rate pressure fairly and uses verified figures. "What the error rate cannot see is a wrongful denial" is an inference from the statutory definition (participating households). It's sound, but note that USDA separately publishes a "case and procedural error rate" for denials and closures (from my background knowledge; I did not fetch a source this session), which the essay doesn't mention and which plays no part in the cost-share formula. That supports the essay's point; the editor may want "is silent on bad denials" changed to "carries no penalty for bad denials" for precision. I left it as is.
