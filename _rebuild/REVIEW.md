## Summary
The facts mostly hold up. The prose has the giveaways. Across the 20 essays we checked about 840 claims: 2 are wrong, about 50 are overstated, misquoted, out of date or missing context, and about 175 could not be confirmed either way, mostly because the checkers hit a search limit.
The writing avoids the obvious tells (no “not just X, it’s Y,” no “delve,” no pile of em dashes). The tells are structural. Every essay is built from the same parts in the same order and lands on the same kind of fix: publish a number, add a column, put a clause in a contract. Read one essay and it seems disciplined. Read five and it reads as a machine.
Fix these first:
- plan-review says the Vital City Just Fix It column is by Melissa O’Shaughnessy. The byline is Vital City; she did the illustration. In our own publication, that is the most embarrassing error in the issue.
- block-by-block proposes matching DOB permits against the cooling-tower registry as if it were a new idea. The City announced exactly that on Sept. 1, 2026. The essay also leaves out the South Bronx Legionnaires’ cluster that began in September.
- fiscal credits the administration with adding $350 million to the general reserve. That was a partial restoration after it had cut the reserve to the $100 million legal minimum.
- deepfakes changes words inside a quotation (the Biden robocall said “save your vote,” not “save their vote”) and gets the order of events wrong in the Cuomo AI-ad story.
- landlord-algorithm suggests DCWP accepted the comptroller’s “research and other leads” recommendation. DCWP rejected it to the extent it would need more resources, and the essay’s argument rests on that recommendation.
- shelter-score says the coordinated entry manual was last posted in 2019; a September 2023 version is posted. Its “8.5%” error-gap figure also appears in no source we could find.
The issue’s About page says every factual statement was traced to a quoted source and checked again. The byline mistake and the misquote show that pipeline still lets errors through, so the claim should be toned down before this goes out.
## Signs of AI writing
Sentence by sentence the prose is clean. At the level of the issue it is formulaic, and a reader who gets through several essays will notice. This section is based on four essays I read in full (front-door, sanctuary-data, fiscal, the-desk) plus the titles of all 20. The other 16 were fact-checked but not read for style, so treat the pattern claims as strong for those four and likely for the rest.
One template, 20 times. Each essay runs: a news hook with a date, the thesis by the end of paragraph three, a plainly labeled section of background evidence, a section titled some version of “The case against” / “The objection,” a concession, then “What the City should do” with First, Second, Third. A good human writer uses this outline sometimes. Twenty in a row reads as a form being filled in.
A scripted counterargument. The objection always gets a stock opener and a stock reply:
- “The strongest objection is that a city-branded bot does its harm out of sight.” (front-door) / “The strongest objection is that saving now has a cost.” (fiscal)
- “A fair objection is that contracts are the wrong fight.” (sanctuary-data)
- “Much of that is right. But…” (sanctuary-data); “Those are good arguments for keeping the job narrow… But…” (front-door); “Some of the worry is warranted.” (the-desk); “The objection has more force against a large reserve target than against saving a windfall.” (fiscal)
The steelman gets granted and then shrunk until the essay’s modest proposal survives it. None of the essays ever loses an argument or changes its mind.
Every essay reaches the same conclusion. Nearly every recommendation is a reporting fix: an accuracy rate in the Mayor’s Management Report, a results column in the Local Law 35 list, a vendor clause, a log, a published table. That reflects caution about going past the sources, and it flattens the issue. Nowhere does the issue argue that the City should stop doing something, spend real money, or pick a fight.
Short summing-up sentences at the end of paragraphs. Many paragraphs close on a crisp short line that restates the point:
- “A skeptic would say the bot made it easier to make.”
- “The scenario with the most job loss is the one the office rates least likely.”
- “Voters would be approving a target, with the mechanics to come.”
- “The cushion is thin.” / “The trouble is what that group lives on.”
- “Either way, a logged chatbot on a caseworker’s screen and a statutory ban… can coexist.”
Once in a while lands. One per paragraph is a tic.
Quoting documents instead of explaining them. The writer had to quote a source for every claim, and it shows: short bureaucratic phrases in quotation marks everywhere (“fully autonomous,” “off-the-shelf,” “select staff,” “input data; output data”), and paragraphs that move from filing to filing (the-desk’s first paragraph cites the Local Law 35 filing four times). It reads as a research memo more than an argument.
Numbers stacked without interpretation. Paragraphs carry four to six figures one after another (fiscal’s “Who pays the income tax” section; front-door’s 311 paragraph) with little help on which number matters. An editor would cut half and say what the remaining ones mean.
No people, no scenes, no voice. The only humans are officials and plaintiffs quoted from news coverage. There is no reporting, no one interviewed, no sense of a writer who has been anywhere or thinks anything surprising. Every essay keeps the same even, measured tone.
Hedging tags pile up. “By the city’s count,” “by his account,” “according to City Hall,” “as the company announced them,” “as of this writing,” “As of Oct. 19.” Each is defensible. Together they make every sentence sound like it is covering itself.
Headlines all built the same way. Of the 20 titles, most are an imperative or a “New York Should”: “Make the Next City Chatbot Show Its Grades,” “Make the NYPD Count Its Surveillance,” “Make the City’s AI Contract Terms Public and Mandatory,” “New York Should Report…,” “New York Should Test…,” “New York’s Child Welfare Algorithm Should Show Its Work.” The table of contents reads like a list of to-dos.
Repeated disclosures and boilerplate. The note that Anthropic’s model wrote the issue comes back in several essays, every essay carries “Illustration generated with Google’s Gemini image model,” and the byline is “A. Bot, AI Assistant.” That is honest, and it also makes the issue feel more like a demo than a magazine.
What it does well. No clichés, no hype, no inflated adjectives, plain section labels, active voice, and named sources on almost every claim. The essays are fair to the other side, perhaps too fair. If the goal is a credible AI-written issue, the fix is editorial: vary the structure, cut half the figures, let some essays argue harder, and drop the closing summary lines.
