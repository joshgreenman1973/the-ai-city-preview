Section I — The Promise

Where AI could earn its keep

The promising tools do narrow jobs: answering routine questions, pre-checking permit plans, matching records so benefits can be approved, pointing a small staff to where trouble is likely and enforcing traffic rules already on the books. Each essay asks what the City would have to measure to know the tool works.

Section II — The Peril

Scoring people, out of public view

Police, courts, child welfare, homeless services, employers and landlords all use software that sorts people. Some of it is published and tested. Much of it is not. The essays ask who can see a score, who can check it and what the City should count.

Section III — The Disruption

What AI is changing outside City Hall

Entry-level office hiring, homework, campaign ads, the power grid and the tax base. The evidence so far is early and often mixed, and these essays say where it runs out.

Section IV — The Governance Question

The rules the City writes for itself

New York City lists its agencies' algorithms every year, has created an office to review them and buys most of its AI under contracts it writes. Washington is fighting the states over rules for AI companies. These essays argue that the City's surest lever is its rules for its own work.

How this issue was made. This is the second version of The Algorithmic City. The first, written in March 2026 by an earlier AI model, leaned on figures nobody had verified and needed repeated corrections. This version was rebuilt from scratch in October 2026 by Claude Opus 5.5, a model made by Anthropic. Anthropic comes up in several essays, and each says so where it does.

The work ran in passes, each done by a separate instance of the model. A writer researched each essay, fetched every source and copied a verbatim quote for each factual claim into a research file; claims it could not confirm stayed out. A fact-checker that had not done the research then went back to the sources for every checkable statement in the draft, __N__ statements across the 20 essays, and corrected __E__ of them. Most were overstatements: a projection stated as a finding, a pilot described as a program, a vendor's claim stated as fact, a date or count gone stale. An editor then revised each essay for clarity and fairness without adding facts, and a final pass reconciled figures that appear in more than one essay.

The charts use data from the essays' research files. The illustrations were generated with Google's Gemini image model. The cover is drawn from the fact-check itself: each window is one checked statement, and the orange ones were corrected.

No person edited these essays before this preview. AI models make confident mistakes, and these checks reduce them without ruling them out. Corrections are welcome.
