On Oct. 15, a federal judge in Brooklyn [threw out](https://www.6sqft.com/federal-judge-rejects-trump-challenge-to-nyc-sanctuary-laws/) the Trump administration's lawsuit against New York City's sanctuary laws, ruling that the government lacked standing and that the Constitution protects cities from being made to spend their resources on federal immigration enforcement. Mayor Zohran Mamdani [said](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/10/statement-from-mayor-mamdani-on-federal-court-dismissal-of-lawsu) the federal government "cannot commandeer our City workers to do its bidding."

The workers are the part the laws protect best. They govern what a City employee may ask, disclose or spend time on. Much of the information that reaches Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) never passes through an employee's hands. It moves through plate camera networks, commercial databases and analytics platforms, under contracts and settings that sanctuary law barely touches.

So the City should stop signing surveillance contracts that leave those settings to the vendor. And the New York Police Department (NYPD) should stop keeping five years of plate reads.

## Where the law stops

On paper, the rules are broad. The Administrative Code [bars](https://intro.nyc/local-laws/2017-228) the use of City resources for immigration enforcement, including time spent by "contractors, or subcontractors while on duty." The 2017 identifying information law [counts](https://intro.nyc/local-laws/2017-247) immigration status, license plate numbers, fingerprints and anything drawn from a police surveillance system as protected data.

That law has two gaps that matter here. It exempts disclosures by or to the police in connection with a criminal investigation. And the contractors it binds are human services providers, plus any others the City's chief privacy officer chooses to designate. A camera vendor sits outside it unless the officer says otherwise.

Mamdani's [Executive Order 13](https://www.nyc.gov/content/dam/nycgov/mayors-office/downloads/pdf/executive-orders/2026/eo-13.pdf), signed Feb. 6, [reaffirms](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/02/at-annual-interfaith-breakfast--mayor-mamdani-reaffirms-city-s-s) that City data cannot go to federal immigration authorities except where the law requires it. It made every agency name a privacy officer and had the NYPD and five other agencies audit how they deal with immigration authorities. It says nothing about how long vendors keep data or who else can search it.

## How plate data travels

In May 2025, 404 Media [reported](https://404media.co/ice-taps-into-nationwide-ai-enabled-camera-network-data-shows) that local and state police had run thousands of searches of Flock Safety's camera network on behalf of federal agents or with a possible immigration focus, though ICE had no contract with the company. Flock's chief executive [later acknowledged](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/ensuring-local-compliance) pilots with Customs and Border Protection and Homeland Security Investigations and said they had been paused.

It happens in New York too. Two advocacy groups, the Surveillance Technology Oversight Project and LatinoJustice PRLDEF, [reported](https://www.stopspying.org/s/2026-09-30_Flock-Favors_STOP-LatinoJustice_Report.pdf) on Aug. 25 that as recently as July, police in Nassau County, Beacon, Glen Cove and Cheektowaga searched Flock with stated reasons such as "Wanted by ICE." They worked from logs they called "an incomplete, redacted snapshot," and some entries seemed to hide their purpose: a state police officer searched a plate an hour after a state crime analysis center had searched it with the reason "SWB 25-03-01 DHS Agent," and listed the reason only as "Investigation."

Syracuse had the contract clause. Its police department [said](https://centralcurrent.org/syracuse-police-exposed-driver-data-to-thousands-of-cops-around-the-country) in 2025 that its Flock contract "explicitly stated" its data was not to be shared outside the department, but that it had "inadvertently" opted into a national network. Officers elsewhere searched Syracuse's data nearly 4.4 million times in about a year.

The NYPD has [never had](https://www.govtech.com/public-safety/nypd-reviews-use-of-flock-safety-ai-license-plate-readers) a Flock contract; it works with another vendor, Vigilant Solutions, and runs its own readers. Its officers searched Flock anyway. Advocates counted 1,710 such searches from March 2023 through December 2025, and more than a third gave no clear reason. Nothing ties them to immigration, and nearly all are believed to involve suspects who fled the city, which is the honest case for sharing: it is how police follow a car across a city line.

The department's [published policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/License-Plate-Readers-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) for its own readers says plate data "is retained for five (5) years" and "is not shared in furtherance of immigration enforcement." It also lets vendors reach the data while performing their contracts. Without search logs that someone outside the department can read, the second promise can't be checked, and any search, authorized or not, reaches back five years.

Some routes no City contract will touch. Fingerprints taken at booking go to the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), which under Secure Communities [sends them automatically](https://www.ice.gov/secure-communities) to the Department of Homeland Security; ICE says localities "cannot prohibit" that. ICE [subscribes](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_70CMSD21C00000001_7012_-NONE-_-NONE-) to LexisNexis's investigative databases, and Palantir has run its case management system [since at least 2022](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_70CTD022FR0000170_7012_GS35F0086U_4730) and in May won a new order for its [enforcement case management system](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_70CTD026FC0000012_7012_70RTAC26A00000001_7001). Federal agencies have fought the states in court over records from the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program ([New York is among the states](https://clearinghouse.net/case/46831/) that won orders blocking that demand), from [Medicaid](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-allows-hhs-to-share-basic-info-with-ice-for-immigration-enforcement/) and from the Internal Revenue Service, which an appeals court [stopped](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/d-c-circuit-blocks-irs-from-sharing-taxpayer-data-with-ice/) in September after tens of thousands of records had gone out. In the Medicaid case, ICE passed data it should not have received to Palantir, according to court filings [reported by NPR](https://www.wrvo.org/2026-07-17/ice-shared-medicaid-data-it-wasnt-supposed-to-have-with-palantir).

Those pipes are bigger than plate cameras. But the state has shown that a rule built into a data system can close one. The Green Light Law requires a warrant before immigration agents get Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) records, and companies that buy DMV records must certify they won't let them be used for immigration enforcement. A University of Washington study [published Oct. 13](https://jsis.washington.edu/humanrights/2026/10/13/dmv-to-dhs-pipeline/) found that "ICE officers are not able to obtain DMV data about cars registered in New York" through the national law enforcement network, and no evidence the DMV was helping. It also found ICE and Customs and Border Protection traffic stops common across the state, and it could not test whether the data broker route was closed. (A Justice Department suit against the law was [dismissed](https://clearinghouse.net/case/46112/) in December; the government has appealed.)

The same study describes the opposite design. An ICE contract required LexisNexis to set up its plate data searches so that even LexisNexis could not know "which of its data had been queried by ICE, and to what end," which leaves no one outside ICE able to check a no-immigration rule.

## What the City should demand

A bill in Albany that would require a federal warrant for plate data and [erase it after 48 hours](https://gothamist.com/news/albany-mulls-new-limits-on-data-from-digital-license-plate-readers) has sat [in committee](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S9890) since April. Washington state [deletes plate data after 21 days](https://www.spokanepublicradio.org/regional-news/2026-03-31/washington-adds-safeguards-for-flock-cameras) and bars immigration use. The City does not have to wait for Albany.

The chief privacy officer can start by designating surveillance and data vendors, beginning with the NYPD's plate reader contracts, as contractors under the identifying information law. Their handling of New Yorkers' data would then answer to the same law as a shelter provider's.

Then the City should refuse to renew any plate reader or data contract that lacks three terms. Retention measured in days or weeks; Flock [says it has cut its own default](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-guardrails-address-lpr-privacy-concerns-and-police-transparency) to seven days. Sharing off by default, with any outside search tied to a stated offense and case number, a filter Flock says it now offers so a city can block "searches related to immigration enforcement." And search logs the City owns and audits, including searches by outside agencies.

The NYPD doesn't need a vendor to shorten its own archive. The usual argument for keeping five years is cold cases, and the department can make that argument with numbers by publishing how often a search of old plate data leads to an arrest. Until it does, the retention period should come down.

Executive Order 13 asked six agencies for one report on their own policies, due May 7. A follow-up order should ask for the vendor logs, on a schedule, starting with the contracts behind the NYPD's five years of plate data.
