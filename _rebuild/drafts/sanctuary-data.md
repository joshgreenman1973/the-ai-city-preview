On Oct. 15, a federal judge in Brooklyn [threw out](https://www.6sqft.com/federal-judge-rejects-trump-challenge-to-nyc-sanctuary-laws/) the Trump administration's lawsuit against New York City's sanctuary laws, ruling that the government lacked standing and that the Constitution protects cities from being made to spend their resources on federal immigration enforcement. Mayor Zohran Mamdani [said](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/10/statement-from-mayor-mamdani-on-federal-court-dismissal-of-lawsu) the court had made clear that the federal government "cannot commandeer our City workers to do its bidding."

The laws govern what a City employee may ask, disclose or spend time on. Much of the information that reaches Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) never passes through an employee's hands. It moves through plate camera networks, commercial databases and analytics platforms, under contracts and settings that sanctuary law barely touches. If the City wants its sanctuary promise to cover data, it should write that promise into its vendor contracts.

## What the laws cover

On paper, New York's rules are broad. The Administrative Code [bars](https://intro.nyc/local-laws/2017-228) the use of City resources, "including, but not limited to, time spent by employees, officers, contractors, or subcontractors while on duty," for immigration enforcement. The 2017 identifying information law [defines](https://intro.nyc/local-laws/2017-247) protected data to include immigration status, "information obtained from any surveillance system operated by, for the benefit of, or at the direction of the police department," license plate numbers and fingerprints.

The identifying information law also has limits. It exempts disclosures "by or to the police department in connection with an investigation of a crime." And the "contractors" it binds are those providing human services, "or other services designated in policies and protocols of the chief privacy officer." A camera vendor falls outside that definition unless the chief privacy officer designates it.

Mamdani's [Executive Order 13](https://www.nyc.gov/content/dam/nycgov/mayors-office/downloads/pdf/executive-orders/2026/eo-13.pdf), signed Feb. 6, reaffirms that City data "cannot be shared with federal immigration authorities, except as required by law," [according to](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/02/at-annual-interfaith-breakfast--mayor-mamdani-reaffirms-city-s-s) City Hall. It gave every agency 14 days to name a privacy officer. It told the New York Police Department (NYPD), the Department of Correction and four other agencies to audit their policies on dealing with immigration authorities and report to the mayor by May 7. Its six pages cover personnel, training, City property and crisis coordination. They say nothing about how long vendors keep data or who else can search it.

## How data reaches immigration agents

In May 2025, 404 Media [reported](https://404media.co/ice-taps-into-nationwide-ai-enabled-camera-network-data-shows) that Flock Safety search logs showed "more than 4,000 nation and statewide lookups by local and state police" done at federal request, as favors to federal agents or with a potential immigration focus. ICE had no contract with Flock; local officers ran the searches. Flock's chief executive [later acknowledged](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/ensuring-local-compliance) pilots with Customs and Border Protection and Homeland Security Investigations and said they had been paused.

New York has its own cases. A report released Aug. 25 by the Surveillance Technology Oversight Project and LatinoJustice PRLDEF, both advocacy groups, [found](https://www.stopspying.org/s/2026-09-30_Flock-Favors_STOP-LatinoJustice_Report.pdf) in search logs it called "an incomplete, redacted snapshot" that as recently as July, police in Nassau County, Beacon, Glen Cove and Cheektowaga had searched Flock's network with stated reasons such as "Immigration (civil/administrative)" and "Wanted by ICE." They also found searches that appeared to hide their purpose: a state police officer searched a plate an hour after a state crime analysis center had searched it with the stated reason "SWB 25-03-01 DHS Agent," and listed the reason only as "Investigation."

Contract language alone did not protect Syracuse. Its police department [said](https://centralcurrent.org/syracuse-police-exposed-driver-data-to-thousands-of-cops-around-the-country) in 2025 that its Flock contract "explicitly stated" its data was not to be shared outside the department, but that it had "inadvertently" opted into a national sharing network. Officers elsewhere searched Syracuse's data nearly 4.4 million times in about a year.

The NYPD has [never had](https://www.govtech.com/public-safety/nypd-reviews-use-of-flock-safety-ai-license-plate-readers) a contract with Flock. It works with another vendor, Vigilant Solutions, and runs its own readers. Even so, advocates found that NYPD officers searched Flock 1,710 times between March 2023 and December 2025, and that 614 of those searches, more than a third, gave no clear reason. Nothing ties them to immigration.

The NYPD's [published policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/License-Plate-Readers-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) for its own plate readers, updated Feb. 4, says plate data "is retained for five (5) years" and that "information is not shared in furtherance of immigration enforcement." Without the search logs, nobody outside the department can check the second promise. The first means any search, authorized or not, can reach back five years.

Other routes run outside City control. When police book someone, the fingerprints go to the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), and under Secure Communities "the FBI automatically sends the fingerprints" to the Department of Homeland Security. ICE's [description](https://www.ice.gov/secure-communities) says state and local jurisdictions "cannot prohibit information-sharing between agencies in this respect." ICE also buys access to commercial records: its LexisNexis investigative database subscription [obligated](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_70CMSD21C00000001_7012_-NONE-_-NONE-) $24.5 million between 2021 and August 2026. Palantir has run ICE's case management system [since at least 2022](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_70CTD022FR0000170_7012_GS35F0086U_4730) under a $170.8 million order; it won a $30 million deal for a tool called ImmigrationOS in April 2025, [Business Insider reported](https://www.yahoo.com/news/ice-just-ordered-30-million-212445164.html), and an $86.3 million order for ICE's [enforcement case management system](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_70CTD026FC0000012_7012_70RTAC26A00000001_7001) in May 2026.

Federal agencies have also gone after benefit and tax records. New York is among the states that [won orders](https://clearinghouse.net/case/46831/) blocking the Agriculture Department's demand for personal data on people in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP); the case is ongoing. A federal judge in San Francisco [allowed](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-allows-hhs-to-share-basic-info-with-ice-for-immigration-enforcement/) Medicaid officials to give ICE basic address and contact data in December, then paused sharing in late May after the government admitted sending more than the order allowed; ICE had passed that data to Palantir, according to court filings [reported by NPR](https://www.wrvo.org/2026-07-17/ice-shared-medicaid-data-it-wasnt-supposed-to-have-with-palantir). In September, a federal appeals court [upheld](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/d-c-circuit-blocks-irs-from-sharing-taxpayer-data-with-ice/) an injunction against the Internal Revenue Service's address-sharing system with ICE, after 47,289 records had already gone out. Those fights are between the federal government and the states, in court, and no City contract reaches them.

## The objection

A fair objection is that contracts are the wrong fight. Plate data helps solve car thefts and shootings, and sharing across jurisdictions is how police track a car that has left the city. A five-year archive helps with cold cases. And the largest pipes, fingerprints at arrest, commercial data brokers and federal benefit and tax records, are beyond any city's reach. On this view, vendor terms are a sideshow.

Much of that is right. The City cannot opt out of Secure Communities, and it should not cripple legitimate investigations. But New York has shown that a rule written into a data system can close at least one channel. The state's Green Light Law requires a warrant before immigration agents get Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) records, and companies that buy DMV records must certify that they will not let them be used for immigration enforcement. A University of Washington study [published Oct. 13](https://jsis.washington.edu/humanrights/2026/10/13/dmv-to-dhs-pipeline/) found that "ICE officers are not able to obtain DMV data about cars registered in New York" through the national law enforcement network, and found no evidence the state DMV was helping. The study also found that ICE and Customs and Border Protection traffic stops are common across New York, and it could not test whether the data broker route was closed. (A Justice Department suit against the law was [dismissed](https://clearinghouse.net/case/46112/) in December; the government has appealed.)

The same study shows what happens when searches are hidden. One ICE contract required LexisNexis to set up plate data searches so even LexisNexis could not know "which of its data had been queried by ICE, and to what end." Under terms like that, no one outside ICE can check whether a no-immigration rule was followed.

## What the City can do

A bill in Albany, stuck in committee since April, would require a federal warrant for plate data and [erase it after 48 hours](https://gothamist.com/news/albany-mulls-new-limits-on-data-from-digital-license-plate-readers). Washington state caps retention at [21 days](https://www.spokanepublicradio.org/regional-news/2026-03-31/washington-adds-safeguards-for-flock-cameras) and bars immigration use. The City does not have to wait for Albany.

Start with a designation. The identifying information law lets the chief privacy officer extend its contractor rules to "other services." The officer can name surveillance and data vendors, starting with the NYPD's plate reader contracts, so their handling of New Yorkers' data falls under the same law as a shelter provider's.

Then write three terms into each of those contracts at renewal. First, a retention limit far shorter than five years; Flock [says it has cut its own default](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-guardrails-address-lpr-privacy-concerns-and-police-transparency) to seven days, from 30. Second, sharing off by default, with any outside search requiring a stated offense and case number, a filter Flock says it now offers so a city can block "searches related to immigration enforcement." Third, search logs the City owns and can audit, including searches by outside agencies, with regular reports to the mayor on what they show.

Executive Order 13 required reports to the mayor only once, by May 7, and only from six agencies about their own policies on immigration authorities. None of it covers vendors. A follow-up order could require those vendor reports on a schedule, starting with the contracts behind the NYPD's five years of plate data.
