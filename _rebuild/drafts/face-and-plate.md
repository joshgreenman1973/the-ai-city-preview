In June 2023, New York City police officers answered 940 alerts from ShotSpotter, the network of street sensors that listens for gunfire. They confirmed 122 as shootings. The comptroller's auditors later estimated that chasing down the rest took about 427 hours of police time that month.

ShotSpotter's contract grades it on something else: whether it misses gunfire. By that test the system did well, passing everywhere but Manhattan in 2022 and in all five boroughs in the first half of 2023. When then-Comptroller Brad Lander [audited the program](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system/), his office found that in the months it sampled, alerts led to confirmed shootings 8% to 20% of the time. The New York City Police Department (NYPD), the auditors wrote, "has the capacity to count" that share but "does not calculate or publish this figure."

The auditors told the department not to renew the contract without a fuller evaluation. The NYPD disagreed, warning that dropping ShotSpotter "may endanger the public" and calling an evaluation before the renewal date "not feasible." In early 2025 it [renewed for three years](https://ir.soundthinking.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/305/soundthinking-announces-a-shotspotter-three-year---renewal), at about $21.8 million by the vendor's figure. The vendor's announcement still described its obligation as the old one, a 90% rate for locating gunfire, which it said it beat in 2024.

That contract [runs through December 2027](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/newsroom/comptroller-lander-declines-to-register-shotspotter-contract-renewal). Lander first declined to register it over procedural defects, and Checkbook NYC, the City's spending database, shows it registered eight days later. The City should not renew ShotSpotter again unless the next contract measures confirmed shootings and the NYPD publishes that count every month.

The system does catch shootings nobody reports. Of the 122 confirmed in June 2023, 33 drew no 911 call. The department also argues that later evidence can turn an unconfirmed alert into a confirmed one, a claim the auditors said "cannot be tested precisely because NYPD does not collect the necessary data." And it told them it was open to a better measure but had "not received a recommended standard." The Council can write one: alerts confirmed at the scene or by later evidence, shootings found with no 911 call and the time spent on the rest.

ShotSpotter is the one NYPD tool whose results an outside auditor has counted. For most of the others the public has rules and no results.

## Rules without counts

The Public Oversight of Surveillance Technology (POST) Act, [signed in July 2020](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2026/08OIGNYPD.AR.Release.Rpt.04.15.2026.pdf), requires the NYPD to publish a policy explaining how it uses each surveillance technology and what that use might do to the public. The department's [POST Act page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/about/about-nypd/policy/post-act.page) listed 41 such policies on Oct. 19, 2026, from body cameras to throwable robots, nearly all rewritten in February.

They describe a great deal and count almost nothing. The [license plate reader policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/License-Plate-Readers-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) says plate data is kept for five years and that some staff can search a national commercial plate database. The [Domain Awareness System policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/Domain-Awareness-System-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) describes the central system through which officers reach camera feeds and those plate reads. Neither says how many readers exist, how many plates they scanned or how often anyone searched the archive. The [drone policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/Unmanned-Aircraft-Systems-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) gives no flight count; the newest public figure comes from the Department of Investigation, which told the Council in [February 2025](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/Testimony/TestimonyPOST_Act_2.19.25_FINAL.pdf) that the NYPD "reportedly" flew more than 4,000 drone missions in 2023. Even the 2025 entries in the City's [inventory of algorithmic tools](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=1000) list the frequency of NYPD facial recognition and ShotSpotter use as "Not specified."

The Department of Investigation's inspector general for the NYPD must audit the policies each year. Its [first POST Act report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2022/20PostActRelease_Rpt_11032022.pdf), in 2022, found most of them too "general and generic" to audit and made 15 recommendations, including periodic audits of facial recognition. The NYPD [at first rejected 14](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2024/25PostActRelease_Rpt_05_30_2024.pdf). After the February rewrite, the [inspector general's April 2026 report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2026/08OIGNYPD.AR.Release.Rpt.04.15.2026.pdf) counted only three of those still rejected. A later request, that each policy include a way to track how each technology is used, remains rejected.

The Council forced the issue for one tool. [Local Law 55](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=831EC8A9-47FF-413B-ADF5-B018DD17282D&ID=14299268&M=F), which became law in May 2025 without Mayor Eric Adams's signature, requires an annual audit of facial recognition with counts of searches, possible matches and no-matches, broken out by type of crime. A [companion law](https://intro.nyc/0480-2024) requires a policy for each distinct technology, naming every outside entity that receives its data. Local Law 55 took effect Sept. 1, 2025, and sets no due date. On Oct. 19, 2026, the City's [list of mandated reports](https://a860-gpp.nyc.gov/mandated_reports/public_list?agency=All&locale=en&page=165&per_page=10) showed the facial recognition audit with no publication date.

## What a facial recognition count would show

In April 2025, according to his lawyer, the NYPD's facial recognition software [misidentified Trevis Williams](https://www.cbsnews.com/newyork/news/nyc-facial-recognition-technology-wrongful-arrest-indecent-exposure), a Brooklyn father, as a man who had exposed himself to a woman in Manhattan. Williams spent two days in custody before the case was dismissed. The man police were looking for was 5-foot-6. "I'm 6-2," Williams told CBS News New York. The Legal Aid Society, which represented him, called it the seventh wrongful arrest tied to facial recognition its lawyers had seen in five years.

In a [letter to the inspector general](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2025-08-25-NYPD-POST-Act-Violations.pdf), Legal Aid said the department's Facial Identification Section had found no match, and that a possible match arrived hours later from a separate unit in the Intelligence Division. The NYPD told CBS it "cannot and will never make an arrest solely using facial recognition technology." Its [facial recognition page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/about/about-nypd/equipment-tech/facial-recognition.page) still says the department knows of no false arrest based on a match, and its newest figures are from 2019: 9,850 requests for comparison and 2,510 possible matches. Nobody outside the department can say whether Williams's case is one bad search in a hundred or one in ten thousand.

The department has a case for the tool. It says facial recognition produced possible matches in 68 murders in 2019. Its [policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/Facial-Recognition-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) treats a match as a lead rather than probable cause, requires a supervisor's review and bars live scanning of crowds. Searches run against arrest and parole photos unless top commanders approve an exception. When the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) tested 189 algorithms in 2019, it [found demographic differences](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2019/12/nist-study-evaluates-effects-race-age-sex-face-recognition-software) in most, with more false matches for Black women in searches like the NYPD's, but the gaps varied widely, and some algorithms, [including one from Idemia](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8280.pdf), one of the NYPD's two vendors, showed no false-match gap NIST could detect. The NYPD's web page says NIST has since found "major improvements in the accuracy of facial recognition software."

Citing its safeguards, the NYPD's policy calls the remaining risks "effectively mitigated." It publishes no number to back that. NIST's [2022 follow-up](https://pages.nist.gov/frvt/reports/demographics/nistir_8429.pdf) found that false-match gaps persist even in high-quality photos, and the 2019 report said agencies should know their own algorithm and, ideally, test it on their own images. NIST's test photos came from government collections, none from surveillance video. The NYPD's searches start with images from cameras at crime scenes.

The Williams case, as Legal Aid tells it, went wrong in the process around the software: a second unit, a second search, then an identification procedure Legal Aid calls unduly suggestive. In a [2024 Columbia protest case](https://www.thecityreporter.nyc/2025/07/18/nypd-fdny-clearview-ai-ban-columbia-palestinian-protest/), the Fire Department ran a Clearview AI search for the NYPD, whose own policy bars that tool, and a judge dismissed the case. Counts broken out by unit and by requesting agency would show whether such detours are rare or routine. Fight for the Future, an advocacy group, [lists about two dozen](https://www.banfacialrecognition.com/map/) places with bans or restrictions on facial recognition. New York's 2025 laws kept the tools and added disclosure rules, and the first disclosure they require has not been posted.

The Council can give the Local Law 55 audit a deadline, such as March 31 for the prior year, and require it to count searches by unit and searches other agencies run for the NYPD. It can amend the POST Act so the plate reader policy reports reads, hits and commercial-database searches each year, and the drone policy reports flights by purpose, with the tables posted on NYC Open Data and checked by the inspector general. For ShotSpotter, the numbers already exist. The auditors built theirs from the NYPD's own internal tracking report and its calls-for-service data on NYC Open Data.
