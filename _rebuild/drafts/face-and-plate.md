In April 2025, according to his lawyer, the New York City Police Department's (NYPD) facial recognition software [misidentified Trevis Williams](https://www.cbsnews.com/newyork/news/nyc-facial-recognition-technology-wrongful-arrest-indecent-exposure), a 36-year-old Brooklyn father, as a man who had exposed himself to a woman in Manhattan. Williams spent two days in custody before the case was dismissed. The man the police were looking for was 5-foot-6. "I'm 6-2," Williams told CBS News New York.

The Legal Aid Society, which represented him, says it was the seventh wrongful arrest tied to facial recognition that its lawyers had seen in five years. In a [letter to the NYPD's inspector general](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2025-08-25-NYPD-POST-Act-Violations.pdf), Legal Aid said the department's Facial Identification Section had found no match, and that a possible match arrived hours later from a separate unit in the Intelligence Division. The NYPD told CBS it "cannot and will never make an arrest solely using facial recognition technology." Its [facial recognition page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/about/about-nypd/equipment-tech/facial-recognition.page) still says the department "knows of no case in New York City in which a person was falsely arrested on the basis of a facial recognition match."

Nobody outside the department can say whether Williams's case is one bad search in a hundred or one in ten thousand. The newest figures on that page are from 2019: 9,850 requests for comparison and 2,510 possible matches.

New York's 2020 surveillance law made the NYPD describe its tools in writing but required no count of how often it uses them. A 2025 amendment requires that count for facial recognition alone, and the City's reports portal still shows no publication date for the first one. The City should require a short annual table for each major tool: how often it was used, what it produced and how often it was wrong.

## The POST Act policies

The Public Oversight of Surveillance Technology (POST) Act, [signed in July 2020](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2026/08OIGNYPD.AR.Release.Rpt.04.15.2026.pdf), requires an "impact and use policy" for every surveillance technology the NYPD uses. As of Oct. 19, 2026, the department's [POST Act page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/about/about-nypd/policy/post-act.page) listed 41 of them, from body cameras to throwable robots. All but one are dated Feb. 4, 2026.

The [license plate reader policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/License-Plate-Readers-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) says plate data is kept for five years and that a limited number of authorized personnel can query a national commercial plate database. The [Domain Awareness System policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/Domain-Awareness-System-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) describes a central repository for camera feeds and those plate reads. Neither says how many readers exist, how many plates they scanned last year or how often anyone searched the archive. The [drone policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/Unmanned-Aircraft-Systems-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf) gives no flight count, though the Department of Investigation told the Council in [February 2025](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/Testimony/TestimonyPOST_Act_2.19.25_FINAL.pdf) that the department "reportedly" flew more than 4,000 drone missions in 2023.

The City's inventory of algorithmic tools, required by Local Law 35, has the same gap. In its [2025 entries](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=1000), the NYPD lists facial recognition and ShotSpotter, a gunshot detection system, and gives the frequency of use for each as "Not specified."

The inspector general for the NYPD, an office in the Department of Investigation, must audit the policies every year. Its [first POST Act report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2022/20PostActRelease_Rpt_11032022.pdf), in November 2022, said most were so "general and generic" that it could not conduct the audit the law calls for. It made 15 recommendations, including periodic audits of the facial recognition unit. The NYPD [rejected 14](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2024/25PostActRelease_Rpt_05_30_2024.pdf).

In April 2025 the Council passed three POST Act amendments. [One](https://intro.nyc/0480-2024) requires a separate policy for each distinct technology, naming every outside entity that receives its data. [Another](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=831EC8A9-47FF-413B-ADF5-B018DD17282D&ID=14299268&M=F), Local Law 55, became law without Mayor Eric Adams's signature. It requires an annual audit of facial recognition use, with counts of comparison requests, possible matches, rejected images, no-matches and searches by type of crime.

The department has since given ground. After its February rewrite, the [inspector general's April 2026 report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2026/08OIGNYPD.AR.Release.Rpt.04.15.2026.pdf) counted the 15 original recommendations as six implemented, five partly implemented or accepted in principle, one under consideration and three still rejected. A separate 2024 recommendation, that every policy include "mechanisms for tracking and monitoring use" of each technology, also remains rejected.

Local Law 55 took effect Sept. 1, 2025, and sets no deadline beyond "annual." On Oct. 19, 2026, the City's [list of mandated reports](https://a860-gpp.nyc.gov/mandated_reports/public_list?agency=All&locale=en&page=165&per_page=10) showed the "Annual Audit of Use of Facial Recognition Technology" with no publication date.

## What the ShotSpotter audit counted

For ShotSpotter, the public count came from the comptroller. In June 2024, then-Comptroller Brad Lander [audited ShotSpotter](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system/), on which the NYPD had spent more than $45 million. In the months the auditors sampled from 2022 and 2023, 8% to 20% of alerts turned out to be confirmed shootings. In June 2023, officers responded to 940 alerts. They confirmed 122 as gunfire, could not confirm 771 and found 47 unfounded. By the auditors' estimate, chasing the unconfirmed and unfounded alerts took 426.9 hours of police time that month.

The auditors found that the NYPD "has the capacity to count the percentage of alerts that are confirmed as shootings" but "does not calculate or publish this figure." The contract's standard was whether the system missed gunfire, and ShotSpotter met its 90% target in every borough but Manhattan.

The NYPD objected that later evidence can turn an unconfirmed alert into a confirmed one. The auditors replied that the claim "cannot be tested precisely because NYPD does not collect the necessary data." In early 2025 the department [renewed the contract](https://ir.soundthinking.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/305/soundthinking-announces-a-shotspotter-three-year---renewal) for three years at about $21.8 million, according to the vendor, which cited a 94% average monthly "accuracy rate" in 2024 under the contract's standard.

## The department's case

Facial recognition produced possible matches in 68 murders and 66 rapes in 2019, according to the NYPD. Under the [department's policy](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/Facial-Recognition-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf), every possible match gets peer and supervisory review, a match is a lead rather than probable cause, searches run against arrest and parole photos unless top commanders approve an exception and the department does no live scanning of crowds.

The research gives the department some support. When the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) tested 189 algorithms from 99 developers in 2019, it [found demographic differences](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2019/12/nist-study-evaluates-effects-race-age-sex-face-recognition-software) in most of them, with more false positives for Black women in one-to-many searches like the NYPD's. But the gaps varied widely by algorithm. NIST expected the most accurate to have smaller gaps, and some, [including one from Idemia](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8280.pdf), one of the NYPD's two vendors, showed no false-positive gap that NIST could detect. The NYPD's web page says NIST has since found "major improvements in the accuracy of facial recognition software." Its policy says it uses only NIST-evaluated algorithms and calls the remaining risks "effectively mitigated."

No published number backs that last claim. NIST's [2022 follow-up](https://pages.nist.gov/frvt/reports/demographics/nistir_8429.pdf) said false-positive gaps, which it called much larger than false-negative ones, occur even in high-quality photos and "must be mitigated by algorithm developers." The 2019 report says it is "incumbent upon the system owner to know their algorithm," and that it will usually be informative to measure "the operational algorithm on the operational image data." NIST's test photos came from government collections, none from video surveillance. The NYPD's searches start with images "captured by cameras at robberies, burglaries, assaults, shootings, and other crimes."

The Williams case, as Legal Aid describes it, went wrong in the process around the software: a second unit, a second search, then an identification procedure Legal Aid calls unduly suggestive. Counts broken out by unit would show whether that happens rarely or often. In a [2024 Columbia protest case](https://www.thecityreporter.nyc/2025/07/18/nypd-fdny-clearview-ai-ban-columbia-palestinian-protest/), the Fire Department ran a Clearview AI search for the NYPD, whose own policy bars that tool, and a judge dismissed the case.

Some places have gone further. Fight for the Future, an advocacy group, [lists about two dozen](https://www.banfacialrecognition.com/map/) cities, towns, counties and states with bans or restrictions on facial recognition, starting with San Francisco in May 2019. Baltimore's expired at the end of 2022. New York's 2025 laws left the NYPD's tools in place and added disclosure rules. A city that keeps the tools needs to know how well they work.

## What the Council can do

The Council can build on Local Law 55. It should give the facial recognition audit a deadline, such as March 31 for the prior calendar year, and require it to break out searches by unit and to count searches other agencies run at the NYPD's request.

It should also amend the POST Act so the policies for its major tools carry the same few lines each year. For ShotSpotter: alerts, confirmed shootings, unfounded alerts and the time officers spent on them. For plate readers: reads, hits and searches of the commercial database. For drones: flights by purpose. The tables should go on NYC Open Data, and the inspector general should check them.

The ShotSpotter renewal, which the comptroller first returned unregistered and which Checkbook NYC shows registered in February 2025, [runs through December 2027](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/newsroom/comptroller-lander-declines-to-register-shotspotter-contract-renewal). The comptroller's auditors recommended accuracy standards that account for the high share of alerts never confirmed as shootings. Any new contract should include one.

The ShotSpotter numbers already exist. The auditors built theirs from the NYPD's internal ShotSpotter tracking report and the department's calls-for-service data on NYC Open Data.
