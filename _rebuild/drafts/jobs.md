Since March 2025, an employer filing a mass-layoff notice with the New York State Department of Labor has been asked whether "technological innovation or automation" contributed to the cuts. An employer who checks the box is asked to [name the technology](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-warn-act-no-ai-related-layoffs-reported-first-year-adding-ai-related).

In the first year, more than 160 companies filed those notices under the state's Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, Amazon and Goldman Sachs among them. None checked the box, a department spokesperson [told Bloomberg Tax](https://news.bloombergtax.com/payroll/ai-related-layoffs-test-new-yorks-ability-to-track-job-losses). By late May 2026, the department told the researchers Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, [one company had: Nespresso](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/why-ai-hasnt-replaced-software-engineers). By the researchers' count, its filing covered 46 people out of roughly 25,000 laid off in the state over that stretch.

The tally fits two stories: artificial intelligence (AI) isn't costing New Yorkers their jobs, or employers won't say that it is. The second has some support. The Legislature never wrote the question into the WARN statute, so employers may not know whether they have to answer it, and they have [no clear definition of "automation" to work from](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-warn-act-no-ai-related-layoffs-reported-first-year-adding-ai-related).

Either way, the box is aimed at the wrong moment. The best evidence so far suggests that AI's first effect on New York City's office workforce shows up in hiring at the bottom of the career ladder, which no layoff notice can record. Gov. Kathy Hochul should sign the AI reporting bill the Legislature passed in June, and the state Labor Department should use it to count the entry-level jobs that never open.

## Fewer seats at the bottom

In a [May report](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/AI-and-NYCs-Fiscal-Future.pdf), City Comptroller Mark Levine's office found that over the 12 months ending in March, New York City college graduates ages 22 to 27 were unemployed at a slightly higher rate than people their age without a degree. It was the first time on record that the graduates came out behind. The report describes a "low-hire, low-fire" economy, in which layoffs stay low and hiring has all but stalled.

Payroll records show something similar nationally. Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen of Stanford's Digital Economy Lab track employment through data from ADP, the payroll processor. In their [August revision](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/), employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI had fallen about 19% behind young workers in less-exposed jobs, a gap that widened over the previous year. Experienced workers showed no such gap, and the shortfall came mostly from hiring fewer young people rather than letting more of them go. The authors are careful about what that proves. The gap shrinks once education is taken into account, some of it opened before ChatGPT, and it runs larger in ADP's records than in government surveys. Their figures describe a pattern and cannot establish its cause, they write.

Others find less. The Budget Lab at Yale, which updates its tracking regularly, [reported on Oct. 14](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/tracking-impact-ai-labor-market) that measures of AI use show no connection to changes in employment or unemployment.

New York City's industry headcounts, the numbers most people watch, mostly look healthy. Federal employment counts for [the Bronx](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36005.csv), [Brooklyn](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36047.csv), [Manhattan](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36061.csv), [Queens](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36081.csv) and [Staten Island](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36085.csv) show computer systems design and the securities industry each up about 7% from March 2023 to March 2026. Accounting and advertising and public relations shrank by more than a tenth, for reasons the counts don't record.

The Census Bureau's Quarterly Workforce Indicators break those jobs down by age. Vital City summed the private-sector figures for the five boroughs through the end of 2025. In the information sector, which includes publishing, broadcasting, telecommunications and data processing, the number of workers ages 22 to 24 fell 31% from 2022 to 2025, while the sector as a whole shrank 6%. In professional, scientific and technical services (law, accounting, consulting, advertising, design), young workers fell 14% as the sector grew slightly.

AI would produce that pattern, and so could other forces. Young workers' share of information jobs was already falling before ChatGPT arrived in late 2022. Professional services firms also cut back on older hires after a busy 2022: hires of 35- to 44-year-olds dropped by about a fifth, against nearly a third for 22- to 24-year-olds. And finance doesn't fit at all. Its young workforce grew about as fast as the industry did.

Nor are New York firms heavy users. Across six rounds of the Census Bureau's [Business Trends and Outlook Survey](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/data_downloads), from mid-July to early October, an average of 17% of firms in the New York metro area said they had used AI in the previous two weeks, against about 23% nationally. That puts New York among the lowest of the 25 metro areas the bureau reports.

Employers' public statements point the other way, though less firmly than they seem to. Challenger, Gray & Christmas, the outplacement firm, counts AI as the reason U.S. employers have given most often for announced job cuts this year, [about a fifth of the total through September](https://www.challengergray.com/blog/job-cuts-fall-in-september-hiring-plans-up-3-over-2025-on-weak-early-seasonal-hiring/). Andy Challenger, the firm's chief revenue officer, has said that naming AI in a layoff announcement ["can win over investors."](https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Challenger-Report-July-2026.pdf) The firm's July report shows how murky a single case can be. Montefiore, the Bronx hospital system, eliminated 12 utilization review nursing positions after adopting new software. The nurses union said AI had replaced them, hospital leaders called that misleading, and Challenger filed the cuts under "possibly AI."

## What the bill would count

The Legislature's bill, [Assembly Bill 9581B](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A9581), would require businesses in New York with more than 50 employees, and publicly traded companies, to report each year how AI affected their staffing. The Labor Department would publish an annual analysis by sector, geography and business size. As of Oct. 19, the bill had not been delivered to Hochul, who [usually decides on major bills](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/politics/2026/09/03/lawmakers-hochul-tech-ai-bills-) in the closing weeks of the year. "We need to understand the data," state Sen. Michelle Hinchey told NY1 in September, urging her to act.

Hochul should sign it. The [bill's text](https://legislation.nysenate.gov/pdf/bills/2025/A9581B) asks employers to estimate how many workers AI displaced, how many it led them to hire and how many "positions previously filled" they decided not to fill because of it. That last question comes closest to a hiring slowdown and may still miss one. Take a hypothetical firm that hires 12 junior analysts this year instead of 20. Read narrowly, the text gives it nothing to report, because the eight seats it skipped were never filled. The department could read the phrase to cover seats filled the year before, but it would have to choose to.

The department should choose to, and go further. The bill lets the department add reporting requirements of its own. It should ask every covered employer how many entry-level positions it filled compared with the year before and whether AI played a part, and it should publish New York City's results separately, which the bill's geographic analysis allows. The Legislature, for its part, should write the automation question into the WARN statute and define its terms. As it stands, the question had drawn a single yes by late May 2026, more than a year in.

The City does not have to wait for Albany. The age-by-industry figures in this essay are free and public. Levine's office, which flagged the young graduates' unemployment in May, could publish the share of jobs held by workers 22 to 24 in the city's information, professional services and finance industries each time the Census Bureau updates its Quarterly Workforce Indicators.
