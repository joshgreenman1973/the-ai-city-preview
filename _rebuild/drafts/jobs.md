Since March 2025, an employer filing a mass-layoff notice with the New York State Department of Labor has faced a new question: did "technological innovation or automation" contribute to the cuts? An employer who checks the box is asked to [name the technology](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-warn-act-no-ai-related-layoffs-reported-first-year-adding-ai-related).

In the first year, more than 160 companies filed those notices under the state's Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, Amazon and Goldman Sachs among them. None checked the box, a department spokesperson [told Bloomberg Tax](https://news.bloombergtax.com/payroll/ai-related-layoffs-test-new-yorks-ability-to-track-job-losses). By late May 2026, the department told the researchers Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, [one company had: Nespresso](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/why-ai-hasnt-replaced-software-engineers). By their count, its filing covered 46 of roughly 25,000 laid-off workers in the state.

The tally invites two readings: artificial intelligence (AI) isn't costing New Yorkers their jobs, or employers won't say that it is. Each has some support. The Legislature never amended the WARN statute to add the question, and employers have [no clear definition of "automation" to work from](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-warn-act-no-ai-related-layoffs-reported-first-year-adding-ai-related). But the box counts firings, and the best evidence so far suggests that AI's first effect on New York City's workforce is a slowdown in hiring at the bottom of office career ladders. The state should be measuring the jobs that never open. A bill the Legislature passed in June could do that with one addition.

## What the hiring data show

In a [May 2026 report](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/AI-and-NYCs-Fiscal-Future.pdf), City Comptroller Mark Levine's office found that in the 12 months ending in March, New York City college graduates ages 22 to 27 had a slightly higher unemployment rate than people their age without a degree, 7.3% to 7.1%. It was the first time on record. The report describes a "low-hire, low-fire" economy, in which hiring has been "moribund" while layoffs stay low.

Payroll records show a similar pattern nationally. Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen of Stanford's Digital Economy Lab track employment using data from ADP, the payroll processor. In their [August 2026 revision](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/), employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI stood about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with young workers in less-exposed jobs, up from 15% a year earlier. Experienced workers show no such gap. The authors find the adjustment happening "primarily through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations."

Industry headcounts in New York City, the numbers most people watch, mostly look healthy. Federal Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages figures, summed across the county files for [the Bronx](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36005.csv), [Brooklyn](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36047.csv), [Manhattan](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36061.csv), [Queens](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36081.csv) and [Staten Island](https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/api/2026/1/area/36085.csv), show computer systems design employment rising from 75,610 in March 2023 to 80,732 in March 2026, and the securities industry from 188,225 to 201,470. Accounting shrank 11% and advertising and public relations 13%, for reasons the figures don't record.

The Census Bureau's Quarterly Workforce Indicators break employment down by age as well as industry and county. Vital City summed the private-sector figures for the five boroughs through the end of 2025. In the information sector, which includes publishing, broadcasting, telecommunications and data processing, the city averaged 12,346 workers ages 22 to 24 in 2022 and 8,553 in 2025, a drop of 31%. Employment in the sector as a whole fell 6%.

In professional, scientific and technical services (law, accounting, consulting, advertising, design), workers 22 to 24 fell 14%, from 31,889 to 27,314, while the sector grew slightly, to 444,799. Their share of the sector's jobs slid from 7.2% to 6.1%.

## The skeptics' case

The Budget Lab at Yale, which updates its tracking regularly, concluded on [Oct. 14](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/tracking-impact-ai-labor-market) that "measures of AI usage show no connection to changes in employment or unemployment" and that its analysis "does not yet clearly indicate an AI-related labor market footprint."

The Stanford authors call their own findings ["descriptive patterns, not causal estimates."](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) The gaps shrink when they account for education, some of the divergence predates ChatGPT, and the gaps run larger in the ADP sample than in national surveys.

New York's figures carry the same warnings. Young workers' share of information jobs was already falling before ChatGPT arrived in late 2022, from 5.4% in 2019 to 4.6% in 2022. Hiring also fell for older workers after a busy 2022: in professional services, hires of 22- to 24-year-olds dropped 30% between 2022 and 2025, and hires of 35- to 44-year-olds fell 21%. And finance doesn't fit the pattern. Its 22- to 24-year-old workforce grew 9%, about as fast as the industry's.

Firms in the New York area also report using AI less than most. In the six rounds of the Census Bureau's [Business Trends and Outlook Survey](https://www.census.gov/hfp/btos/data_downloads) collected from mid-July to early October, an average of 17% of firms in the New York metro area said they had used AI in the previous two weeks, against about 23% nationally. That puts New York among the lowest of the 25 metro areas in the bureau's file. Its average sits about one point below the next-lowest, the Riverside, Calif., area, a gap that may fall within the survey's sampling error.

National layoff announcements point the other way, though less firmly than they seem to. Challenger, Gray & Christmas, the outplacement firm, counts AI as the leading reason U.S. employers have given for announced job cuts this year: [120,136 through September](https://www.challengergray.com/blog/job-cuts-fall-in-september-hiring-plans-up-3-over-2025-on-weak-early-seasonal-hiring/), about 21% of the total. Andy Challenger, the firm's chief revenue officer, has said that ["naming AI in a layoff announcement can win over investors."](https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Challenger-Report-July-2026.pdf) Individual cases are murky. In its July report, the firm described Montefiore, the Bronx hospital system, eliminating 12 utilization review nursing positions after adopting new software. The nurses union said AI had replaced them, hospital leaders called that "misleading," and Challenger filed the cuts under "Technological Update (possibly AI)."

Taken together, the evidence shows young workers losing ground in parts of New York City's office economy, in the pattern AI would produce, without proving that AI caused it. Forecasts don't settle the question. In the darkest of the comptroller's planning scenarios, to which his office assigns a 5% probability, private employment in the city falls as far as 259,000 jobs below the baseline in early 2029. The report calls its scenarios ["not firm predictions."](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/AI-and-NYCs-Fiscal-Future.pdf)

## The reporting bill

In June, the Legislature passed [Assembly Bill 9581B](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A9581), which would require businesses in New York with more than 50 employees, and publicly traded companies, to report each year on how AI affected their staffing. The Labor Department would publish an annual analysis by sector, geography and business size. As of Oct. 19, the bill had not been delivered to Gov. Kathy Hochul, who [usually decides on major bills](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/politics/2026/09/03/lawmakers-hochul-tech-ai-bills-) in the closing weeks of the year.

She should sign it. The [bill's text](https://legislation.nysenate.gov/pdf/bills/2025/A9581B) asks employers to estimate how many workers AI displaced, how many it led them to hire and how many "positions previously filled" they decided not to fill because of it. That last category comes closest to a hiring slowdown, and it may still miss one. Take a hypothetical firm that hires 12 junior analysts this year instead of 20. Read narrowly, the text gives it no previously filled position to report, because the eight seats it skipped were never filled. The Labor Department could read the phrase more broadly, to count seats filled the year before, and it would have to choose.

The bill already lets the department "develop additional reporting requirements." The department should use that power to ask employers how many entry-level positions they filled compared with the year before, and whether AI played a part. It should publish New York City's results separately, which the bill's requirement to analyze by geographic location allows.

The City does not have to wait for Albany. The age-by-industry figures in this essay are free and public. Levine's office, which reported the young graduates' unemployment figures in May, could publish the share of jobs held by workers 22 to 24 in the city's information, professional services and finance industries each time the Census Bureau updates its Quarterly Workforce Indicators.
