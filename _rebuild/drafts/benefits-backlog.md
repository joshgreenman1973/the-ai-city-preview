Among the terms New York City accepted to settle a class action over late food stamps is a pop-up window. Before a worker at the Human Resources Administration (HRA) denies or closes a case for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) or cash assistance, the screen must [prompt the worker "to check HRA systems for documents"](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026-3-11-Forest-Revised-Stipulation-of-Settlement-FINAL.pdf) the applicant may already have sent. The settlement gives the reason: "To prevent erroneous denials or closures when documents are in HRA's possession." Another clause bars rejecting an application for missing paperwork when the paperwork has been scanned but not yet attached to the case.

The Legal Aid Society, the New York Legal Assistance Group and the law firm Dechert sued in January 2023, arguing that HRA was failing to process SNAP and cash assistance applications [within the 30 days federal and state law require](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Legal-Aid-NYLAG-and-Dechert-LLP-Reach-Landmark-Settlement-Ending-Chronic-Delays-in-SNAP-and-Cash-Assistance-Processing.pdf). In July 2023 a federal judge ordered the City into full compliance. The settlement is still before Judge Jennifer Rearden. Its [public notice](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/3.31-Forest-Class-Settelment-Notice-FINAL.pdf) set a fairness hearing for June 30, and as of mid-October no final approval order had been made public and the case remained open.

A federal law is now adding verification work to benefits offices across the country, and Washington is urging states to use software to do it. Across the country, automation has helped poor people when it gathers and checks their paperwork, and it has hurt them badly when it has been allowed to cut them off.

New York should write that line into the rules HRA works under. A tool may find, file, verify and approve on its own. A denial, closure or sanction must come from a worker who has looked at the case file.

## HRA's on-time rates

In fiscal 2023, HRA processed [39.7%](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/hra.pdf) of SNAP applications on time. In fiscal 2026, which ended June 30, it processed 84.7%, three percentage points below the year before. Cash assistance rose more slowly over the same years, from 28.8% to 65.8%. The target for both is 95%.

Explaining the cash assistance gain, the Mayor's Management Report says that to improve timeliness HRA implemented "a new technology tool to streamline the process of assigning cases for review." HRA is also testing a tool that "flags discrepancies and potential errors for staff" and says it is working to "automate application processing." The first two tools hand their output to workers. One routes cases to reviewers, and the other points out problems for them to check.

Workers make the eligibility calls now, and the appeals record is poor. HRA clients requested 187,602 state fair hearings in fiscal 2026. In the hearings that ended in a decision, the state upheld HRA [13.6% of the time](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/hra.pdf). That figure covers hearings across all of HRA's programs, of which SNAP is one. A tool trained on those decisions, or one that sends out notices faster than workers can, would repeat their mistakes at higher volume.

## What the federal law requires

H.R. 1, which became law on [July 4, 2025](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm), narrowed SNAP's work-rule exemption for parents to those with a child under 14. The Agriculture Department's implementing guidance reads the law as raising the upper age covered by the work rules [from 54 to 64](https://www.usda.gov/sites/default/files/guidance-documents/fns.snap-obbb-abawd-exceptions-implementation.pdf). New York City's SNAP rolls fell 7% in fiscal 2026, to 1.68 million people, and the City ties the decline to a national drop driven by the law.

The law also moves costs to the states. Since Oct. 1, the federal government has paid [25% of SNAP administrative costs](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm), down from 50%. Starting in federal fiscal 2028, states with a payment error rate of 10% or more pay 15% of the benefits themselves. New York's rate for fiscal 2025 was [13.18%](https://www.fns.usda.gov/sites/default/files/resource-files/snap-qcfy25-per.pdf). The law gives a one-year reprieve, to fiscal 2029, to states whose 2025 rate multiplied by 1.5 reaches 20%. New York's comes to 19.77. (A state whose fiscal 2026 rate clears the same bar gets two years.) Hunger Solutions New York, an advocacy group, puts the largest possible cost to the state at [an estimated $1.2 billion a year](https://hungersolutionsny.org/?p=24220). A one-year delay in the Senate's farm bill [cleared the Agriculture Committee](https://wlos.com/news/nation-world/mcconnell-returns-to-back-senate-farm-bill-as-panel-advances-it-on-party-line-vote) in September but had not passed the full Senate as of mid-October.

Medicaid's new rules demand more checking still. By Jan. 1, 2027, adults ages 19 to 64 covered through the Medicaid expansion must show [80 hours a month](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/medicaid-community-engagement-requirement-certain-individuals-interim-final-rule-comment-period-cms) of work, school or service, verified at application and at renewal, and their renewals move to every six months. Someone the state cannot verify gets 30 days to prove compliance or lose coverage. The state now runs most Medicaid administration in New York City, so these checks will mostly run through state systems.

For Medicaid, Congress wrote the helpful kind of automation into the statute. States must [use "reliable information available to the State (such as payroll data ...)"](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm) to verify compliance "without requiring, where possible, the applicable individual to submit additional information." The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services is offering [$200 million in grants](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/cms-launches-nationwide-framework-implement-medicaid-work-requirements) and cites "more than $600 million in committed support from private-sector technology vendors" to build the systems.

The Medicaid "unwinding" after the pandemic tested both uses of software at national scale. At least 25.2 million people lost coverage, and [69% of them](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/medicaid-enrollment-and-unwinding-tracker/) lost it for procedural reasons such as an unreturned form, according to KFF. Over the same period, 61% of renewals went through automatically, with no action by the enrollee. Record matching kept most renewals moving, and paperwork accounted for most of the losses.

## When software makes the call

Michigan's unemployment agency spent [$44,400,558](https://spectrum.ieee.org/michigans-midas-unemployment-system-algorithm-alchemy-that-created-lead-not-gold) on a system called MiDAS. From October 2013 to September 2015, according to a review reported in IEEE Spectrum, the system decided 40,195 fraud cases with no human involvement, and 85% of those findings were wrong. Penalties ran to 400% of the amount claimed as fraud.

In Arkansas, a home-care algorithm the state began using in 2016 cut Tammy Dobbs, who has cerebral palsy, [from 56 hours of care a week to 32](https://www.theverge.com/2018/3/21/17144260/healthcare-medicaid-algorithm-arkansas-cerebral-palsy), The Verge reported. The Verge also reported that cerebral palsy had not been coded properly in the algorithm, a flaw that "caused incorrect calculations for hundreds of people, mostly lowering their hours." The vendor's president called it "an error on our part." The Verge did not say whether the error figured in Dobbs's own cut.

The same Michigan review found that 22,589 fraud cases with some human involvement had a 44% false rate. So a rule that only puts a person somewhere in the chain will catch little. It has to require the worker to open the file and see the evidence.

## The case for automated denials

The best argument against such a rule is the error-rate penalty. Overpayments made up 12.06 points of New York's 13.18%. A state facing a possible billion-dollar bill will want tools that catch ineligible cases, and HRA has already introduced what it calls a payment integrity tool. Its SNAP "cost avoidance for fraud and abuse" nearly doubled last year, to [$95.04 million](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/hra.pdf). Requiring a worker to review every denial costs staff time, and since Oct. 1 Washington has paid a quarter of that cost instead of half.

The rule leaves room for those tools. A payroll match that confirms someone is working can also show that someone earns too much, and a worker can act on it. But federal law measures the error rate on a sample of [participating households](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/2025). A family wrongly turned away is not participating, so it never enters the count. The cost-share formula carries no penalty for bad denials, so a written rule has to cover them.

## A rule HRA could adopt now

Code for America's April 2026 review of state use of artificial intelligence rated [no state "advanced"](https://files.codeforamerica.org/2026/04/30110659/government-ai-landscape-assessment-2026-research-report-final.pdf) on putting it into operation or on measuring its effect. It found that first deployments tend toward "backlog reduction, document processing, fraud detection, eligibility triage" and internal workflow. In New York City, the Department of Social Services' 2025 filing under the City's algorithmic tools law lists [five tools](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=500), none used to decide SNAP or cash eligibility. The City has time to set the rule before such tools arrive.

The Forest settlement already applies the rule to one screen. The City's social services commissioner could adopt it as standing policy for every tool, present and future. Software may sort, index, pre-fill, match records and approve. Software may flag a case, but no denial, closure or sanction notice goes out on a flag until a worker has opened the documents on file, and the notice must name the specific item or fact that is missing.

The state should apply the same rule to the Medicaid work checks it begins in January.

HRA should also add two counts to the monthly reports the settlement calls for it to give plaintiffs' lawyers: how many adverse actions involved an automated flag, and how many of those were reversed at a fair hearing. Those reports already cover case processing, timeliness and call wait times, so the new counts would go to the same lawyers on the same schedule.
