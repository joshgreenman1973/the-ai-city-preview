Among the terms New York City accepted to settle a class action over late food stamps is a pop-up window. Before a worker at the Human Resources Administration (HRA) denies or closes a case for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) or cash assistance, the screen must [prompt the worker to search HRA's systems](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026-3-11-Forest-Revised-Stipulation-of-Settlement-FINAL.pdf) for documents the applicant may already have sent. Another clause stops workers from rejecting an application for missing paperwork that has been scanned but not yet attached to the case. The settlement says both are meant to stop wrongful denials when HRA already has the documents.

The Legal Aid Society, the New York Legal Assistance Group and the law firm Dechert sued in January 2023 because HRA kept missing the [30-day deadline federal and state law set](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Legal-Aid-NYLAG-and-Dechert-LLP-Reach-Landmark-Settlement-Ending-Chronic-Delays-in-SNAP-and-Cash-Assistance-Processing.pdf) for deciding applications. That July a federal judge ordered the City into full compliance. The settlement's [public notice](https://legalaidnyc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/3.31-Forest-Class-Settelment-Notice-FINAL.pdf) set a fairness hearing before Judge Jennifer Rearden for June 30, and by mid-October no final approval order had been made public.

The agency has since gotten much faster. In fiscal 2023 it processed [39.7%](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/hra.pdf) of SNAP applications on time. In fiscal 2026 it processed 84.7%, against a target of 95%, and cash assistance improved more slowly. The tools HRA describes in its latest Mayor's Management Report hand their work to people: one assigns cases for review, and another, still being tested, flags discrepancies for staff to check. The agency says it is working to automate application processing next.

The people still get a lot wrong. When HRA clients took a case to a state fair hearing in fiscal 2026 and got a decision, the state sided with HRA [only 13.6% of the time](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/hra.pdf), across all of the agency's programs. A tool trained on those decisions, or one that sends notices faster than workers can, would repeat their mistakes at higher volume.

Now a federal law is adding verification work to every benefits office in the country, and Washington is urging states to use software to do it. Automation has helped poor people when it gathers and checks their paperwork and hurt them when it has been allowed to cut them off. New York should write that line into HRA's rules. A tool may find, file, verify and approve on its own. A denial, closure or sanction must come from a worker who has looked at the case file.

## Washington's new checks

H.R. 1, signed on [July 4, 2025](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm), narrowed SNAP's work-rule exemption for parents to those with a child under 14, and the Agriculture Department reads it as extending the work rules to [adults up to 64, from 54](https://www.usda.gov/sites/default/files/guidance-documents/fns.snap-obbb-abawd-exceptions-implementation.pdf). New York City's SNAP rolls shrank in fiscal 2026, and the City ties the drop to a national decline driven by the law.

The law also shifts costs to the states. Since Oct. 1, Washington has paid [a quarter of SNAP's administrative costs](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm) instead of half. Starting in federal fiscal 2028, any state whose payment error rate is 10% or higher pays 15% of its benefits. New York's rate for fiscal 2025 was [13.18%](https://www.fns.usda.gov/sites/default/files/resource-files/snap-qcfy25-per.pdf), just under the level that would have bought it a year's delay.

Hunger Solutions New York, an advocacy group, puts the largest possible cost to the state at [about $1.2 billion a year](https://hungersolutionsny.org/?p=24220). The farm bill the Senate Agriculture Committee [passed on a party-line vote](https://wlos.com/news/nation-world/mcconnell-returns-to-back-senate-farm-bill-as-panel-advances-it-on-party-line-vote) in September would [push the benefit cost share back a year](https://www.naco.org/news/senate-agriculture-committee-passes-farm-bill), but as of mid-October it had not passed the full Senate.

That penalty will pull HRA toward software that says no. Overpayments made up [12.06 points](https://www.fns.usda.gov/sites/default/files/resource-files/snap-qcfy25-per.pdf) of New York's rate. HRA has already introduced what it calls a payment integrity tool, and the SNAP fraud and abuse costs it reports avoiding [nearly doubled](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/hra.pdf) last year. A worker reviewing every denial costs staff time, and Washington now pays less of it. But federal law measures the error rate on a sample of [participating households](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/2025). A family wrongly turned away never enters the count, so the formula penalizes a state for paying too much and never for refusing people who qualify.

The City should pay for the staff time. Fraud tools still have a place under the rule. A payroll match that shows someone earns too much goes to a worker, who can act on it. What the rule forbids is a flag that turns into a closure notice with no one looking.

Medicaid's new rules demand more checking. By Jan. 1, 2027, adults covered through the Medicaid expansion must show [80 hours a month](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/medicaid-community-engagement-requirement-certain-individuals-interim-final-rule-comment-period-cms) of work, school or service, and their renewals move to every six months. The state runs most Medicaid administration in New York City now, so these checks will mostly go through state systems. Congress told states to verify compliance with [data they already hold, such as payroll records](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm), and to avoid asking people for more paperwork where possible. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services has announced [more than $600 million in support pledged by private technology vendors](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/cms-launches-nationwide-framework-implement-medicaid-work-requirements) to help states rebuild their eligibility systems.

The Medicaid "unwinding" after the pandemic ran both kinds of software at national scale. Of the people whose coverage was renewed, [61% were renewed automatically](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/medicaid-enrollment-and-unwinding-tracker/), without having to do anything. Of the more than 25 million who lost coverage, 69% lost it for procedural reasons such as an unreturned form, and KFF, the health policy research group, warns that many of them may still have been eligible.

## When software decides

Michigan's unemployment agency let a system called [MiDAS](https://spectrum.ieee.org/michigans-midas-unemployment-system-algorithm-alchemy-that-created-lead-not-gold) decide fraud cases by itself. A review reported in IEEE Spectrum found that from October 2013 to September 2015 it made 40,195 fraud findings with no human involvement, and 85% were wrong. The penalties ran to four times the amount claimed.

A person in the loop did not save those cases. In the 22,589 Michigan cases where someone had a hand in the fraud finding, 44% of the findings were false. A rule that asks only for a human signature somewhere in the chain will catch little. It has to make the worker open the file and look at the evidence.

In Arkansas, a home-care algorithm the state adopted in 2016 cut Tammy Dobbs, who has cerebral palsy, [from 56 hours of care a week to 32](https://www.theverge.com/2018/3/21/17144260/healthcare-medicaid-algorithm-arkansas-cerebral-palsy). The Verge also reported that the algorithm had not coded cerebral palsy properly, a mistake the vendor's president called its own, which miscalculated care for hundreds of people and mostly lowered their hours. The Verge did not say whether the error figured in Dobbs's cut.

## A rule for every screen

New York can set the rule before the tools arrive. Code for America's April 2026 review of state use of artificial intelligence rated [no state "advanced"](https://files.codeforamerica.org/2026/04/30110659/government-ai-landscape-assessment-2026-research-report-final.pdf) at putting it into operation or at measuring what it does, and found that early systems tend to go after backlogs, document processing, fraud detection and eligibility triage. The Department of Social Services' 2025 filing under the City's algorithmic tools law lists [five tools](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=500), none used to decide SNAP or cash eligibility.

The Forest settlement already applies the rule to one screen. The City's social services commissioner should make it standing policy for every tool HRA runs now or buys later. Software may sort, index, pre-fill, match records and approve. It may flag a case, but no denial, closure or sanction notice should go out on a flag until a worker has opened the documents on file, and the notice should name the specific item or fact that is missing. HRA should turn down any vendor contract that will not let it work that way.

The state should hold its Medicaid work checks, which begin in January, to the same rule. A failed payroll match should send the case to a worker before any notice of noncompliance goes out.

HRA should also add two counts to the monthly reports the settlement calls for it to give plaintiffs' lawyers: how many adverse actions involved an automated flag, and how many of those were reversed at a fair hearing. Those reports already cover case processing, timeliness and call wait times.
