On Jan. 3, 2023, New York City's Education Department [blocked ChatGPT](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2023/1/3/23537987/nyc-schools-ban-chatgpt-writing-artificial-intelligence/), the artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot, on school devices and networks. A department spokesperson, Jenna Lyle, said the tool "does not build critical-thinking and problem-solving skills." In the same Chalkbeat story, Adam Stevens, a history teacher at Brooklyn Technical High School, argued that the best way to keep students off ChatGPT was "assigning them work that is inviting them to explore things worth knowing."

Nearly four years later, the City has acted on Lyle's worry for younger children and has barely touched Stevens' idea. On Sept. 2, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Chancellor Kamar Samuels [announced](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-and-chancellor-samuels-put-students-first-with-nat) a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI from 2-K through eighth grade, covering nearly 600,000 students, about two-thirds of enrollment. "The tech industry wants us to believe that A.I.-powered early education is not only inevitable, but necessary," Mamdani said. "We do not see it that way."

For young children, the pause is a reasonable bet, and nothing in the research argues against it. But it does nothing about the problem high school teachers have lived with since 2023: take-home work that a chatbot can write. The Education Department should tell high schools in writing that an AI detector's score is not enough to prove cheating, and it should require take-home essays to come with work done in class.

## How the City got here

The 2023 block was porous. Schools could request access, and students could open ChatGPT on a phone or a home network. In May 2023, Chancellor David Banks [wrote](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2023/5/18/23727942/chatgpt-nyc-schools-david-banks/) that "the knee-jerk fear and risk overlooked the potential of generative AI." That same month the department started using two chatbots it had built in-house on Microsoft technology, an Algebra Teaching Assistant and a tool called "Open Gen AI," according to the City's [inventory of algorithmic tools](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=500), which Local Law 35 of 2022 requires.

## What the pause does

The new rules cover any software with student-facing generative AI through eighth grade, and companion chatbots are banned in every grade. Teachers may still use AI for planning, though [the NYC Public Schools guidance](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/policies/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence) bars it from grading. Assistive technology for students with disabilities and multilingual learners is exempt, as are career programs like computer science. Officials [said](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/02/what-to-know-about-nyc-public-schools-generative-ai-ban-screen-time-limits/) they are disabling AI features in 38 software programs but have not said which ones, and admitted having little data on how much AI is in use across 1,600 schools.

Every high school student takes two 45-minute AI literacy modules a year. Up to 50,000 of them, roughly 17% of high schoolers by Chalkbeat's count, can join one of five pilots: Quill for English, Edia for math, Brisk Teaching, Playlab and Intel AI-Ready Schools. A school may run at most five pilot classes, each supervised by a trained teacher. A Technology in Schools Coalition is due to report in April 2027.

Many parents [welcomed the rules](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/10/nyc-parents-applaud-back-to-school-ai-screen-time-limits/). Critics came from both directions. Richard Buery, the Robin Hood Foundation's chief executive and a former deputy mayor, called "a blanket one-year AI ban, without exceptions" through middle school "not the right call." The AI Moratorium Coalition, which wanted two years, [called the high school pilots](https://www.k12dive.com/news/new-york-city-pauses-ai-use-for-elementary-middle-school-students/829496/) "dangerous." The companion screen-time rules are being applied case by case: on Oct. 9, Chalkbeat [reported the first exemption](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/10/09/cuny-reading-fellows-school-mamdani-device-ban-exception/) to the device ban for young students, for human tutors who teach by video in a City University of New York reading program.

## What the studies say

In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey, Hamsa Bastani and colleagues [gave some students](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html) a plain GPT-4 chat window. Their practice grades rose 48%. When the tool was taken away, they scored 17% lower on an exam than students who never had it. A tutor version with safeguards "largely mitigated" the damage. Without guardrails, the authors wrote, students used the chatbot as a "crutch."

A World Bank [randomized trial in Nigeria's Edo State](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/11125.html) put first-year senior secondary students in English sessions with Microsoft Copilot for six weeks in 2024, with teacher support, and found gains of 0.23 standard deviation in English. At Harvard, students in an introductory physics course who worked at home with a [carefully prompted AI tutor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6) posted median gains more than double those of students in an active-learning class.

So a carefully designed tutor, or a chatbot used with a teacher's support, can help, and an open chatbot with no guardrails can hurt. All three trials involved high school or college students. None tested young children, so the evidence offers no case for putting a chatbot in front of a second grader, and waiting a year costs little. The City says it chose the pilots for "keeping students as the primary thinker," and most are used briefly under a teacher's supervision, which resembles the conditions in which the trials found gains.

## Homework and cheating

The word "homework" appears once on the City's guidance page, in a note that the Edia pilot is for "in-class practice only, never homework." The page never mentions cheating, academic integrity or plagiarism. Chalkbeat noted that the new guidance does not seem to say any more than the department's March draft did about students using AI outside school, including to cheat on writing. Samuels said schools would need to enlist parents.

In a [Pew Research Center survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/) of 1,458 teens nationwide in fall 2025, one in 10 said they do all or most of their schoolwork with a chatbot's help, and 21% said some. Fifty-nine percent said AI cheating happens at least somewhat often at their school.

Detection software is the obvious response. In a [2023 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10382961/) by Weixin Liang and colleagues at Stanford, seven popular detectors correctly classified 88 essays by American eighth-graders but labeled more than half of 91 essays by non-native English writers as machine-made, an average false-positive rate of 61.3%. At least one detector flagged 97.8% of those essays. The study tested 2023 versions of the tools on small samples. Still, in 2025-26, [15.4%](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/reports/doe-data-at-a-glance) of the city's 884,644 public school students were English language learners, and a tool that misreads their writing the way those detectors did could turn honest work into disciplinary cases. More than a third of states already tell schools not to rely on detectors alone, and half of the 10 largest districts discourage them outright, [Chalkbeat found](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/09/02/state-ai-guidance-schools-skirts-cheating-teachers/).

Some New York City schools have written their own rules. East Side Community School in Manhattan drafted a 12-page policy that [prohibits](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/03/23/schools-develop-ai-policies-awaiting-city-guidance/) "the unsupervised use of generative AI for all schoolwork and assessments." Brooklyn Collaborative asks teachers to mark each assignment with a traffic light: green for permitted uses, yellow for caution, red for off limits. Jessica Radin, a social studies teacher at the Beacon School, told Chalkbeat in March that she had moved all writing into class the previous semester. "I have gone back to paper," she said. Stevens, still at Brooklyn Tech, tries to pose questions with a personal component that students will find "too important for them to feed into a chatbot."

## The case for catching cheaters

Paige Wyatt, a former high school English teacher interviewed by Chalkbeat, spent her own money on detection tools and asked for "real consequences." Moving writing into class has costs: Chalkbeat's reporting found shorter essays that use up more instructional time. Redesigning assignments takes teacher time. And the research on what works is thin. "We do not know what to do. There's no evidence-based policy," said Justin Reich of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

Wyatt is right that cheating should have consequences. But a penalty needs evidence that holds up when a parent challenges it, and a detector score is shaky evidence when detectors tested in 2023 misread most essays by non-native English writers. A draft written in class, or a five-minute conversation about an essay, is evidence a teacher can stand behind. Christopher Harris, who directs the school library system for a regional education agency in western New York, [told Chalkbeat](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/09/02/state-ai-guidance-schools-skirts-cheating-teachers/), "The only control they have is their agency in developing assignments."

## What the City can do this year

The Education Department can make two changes before the coalition reports in April 2027.

First, it can tell every high school in writing that a score from an AI detector is not enough on its own to find that a student cheated. A memo costs little and protects the students those detectors misread most often.

Second, it can add a line to the high school guidance: a take-home essay counts toward a grade only if it comes with a draft written in class or a short conference with the teacher. Some teachers, like Radin, already go further. East Side's policy and Brooklyn Collaborative's traffic lights take different approaches and do not require in-class work, but the department could post them as examples of schools that wrote their own rules. The coalition's April report can then say how many high schools adopted the essay rule and what their teachers saw.
