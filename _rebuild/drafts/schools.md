Jessica Radin, a social studies teacher at the Beacon School in Manhattan, [told Chalkbeat](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/03/23/schools-develop-ai-policies-awaiting-city-guidance/) in March that she had moved all of her students' writing into class the semester before. "I have gone back to paper," she said. Many English and social studies teachers in the city have done the same, and Chalkbeat found the cost: shorter essays that eat up more class time. East Side Community School in Manhattan drafted a 12-page policy that prohibits "the unsupervised use of generative AI for all schoolwork and assessments." At Brooklyn Collaborative, teachers mark each assignment with a traffic light, green for permitted uses of AI, yellow for caution and red for off limits.

Those schools wrote their own rules while they waited for the Education Department. When the department's rules came, they were written mostly for younger children. On Sept. 2, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Chancellor Kamar Samuels [announced](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-and-chancellor-samuels-put-students-first-with-nat) a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative artificial intelligence (AI) from 2-K through eighth grade, covering nearly 600,000 students, and banned companion chatbots in every grade. "The tech industry wants us to believe that A.I.-powered early education is not only inevitable, but necessary," Mamdani said. "We do not see it that way."

For young children the pause is a sound call, and nothing in the research argues against it. For high schools it leaves the problem teachers like Radin have been handling alone. The City's [guidance page](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/policies/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence) uses the word "homework" once, to say that a math pilot called Edia is for "in-class practice only, never homework." It never mentions cheating, plagiarism or academic integrity. The department should bar schools from treating an AI detector's score as proof that a student cheated, and it should require that any take-home essay counted toward a grade come with a draft written in class or a short conference with the teacher.

The department has changed its mind about chatbots before. In January 2023 it [blocked ChatGPT](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2023/1/3/23537987/nyc-schools-ban-chatgpt-writing-artificial-intelligence/) on school devices and networks, and a spokesperson said the tool "does not build critical-thinking and problem-solving skills." Students could still open it on a phone or at home. By May, Chancellor David Banks [wrote](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2023/5/18/23727942/chatgpt-nyc-schools-david-banks/) that "the knee-jerk fear and risk overlooked the potential of generative AI," and that month the department began using two chatbots of its own, built on Microsoft technology, according to the City's [inventory of algorithmic tools](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=500). In the story about the 2023 block, Adam Stevens, a history teacher at Brooklyn Technical High School, said the best way to keep students off ChatGPT was "assigning them work that is inviting them to explore things worth knowing." This March he was still at Brooklyn Tech, writing prompts with a personal component that students would find "too important for them to feed into a chatbot."

Teachers in every grade may still use AI to plan lessons but not to grade. Every high school student gets two short AI literacy classes a year, and up to 50,000, about 17% of high schoolers by Chalkbeat's count, can join one of five pilots, each supervised by a trained teacher. A Technology in Schools Coalition is due to report in April 2027. Many parents [welcomed the rules](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/10/nyc-parents-applaud-back-to-school-ai-screen-time-limits/). Richard Buery, the Robin Hood Foundation's chief executive and a former deputy mayor, called "a blanket one-year AI ban, without exceptions" through middle school "not the right call," while the AI Moratorium Coalition, which wanted two years, [called the high school pilots](https://www.k12dive.com/news/new-york-city-pauses-ai-use-for-elementary-middle-school-students/829496/) "dangerous."

The best-known experiment shows what an unguarded chatbot does to learning. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey, Hamsa Bastani and colleagues [gave some of them](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html) an open GPT-4 chat window. Their practice grades rose 48%, and when the tool was taken away they did 17% worse on an exam than students who never had it. They had used it as a "crutch," the authors wrote. A tutor version with safeguards largely avoided the damage.

Other trials found gains under tighter conditions. A World Bank [randomized trial in Nigeria](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/11125.html) gave secondary students six weeks of English sessions with Microsoft Copilot and teacher support, and their English scores improved. At Harvard, introductory physics students who worked at home with a [carefully prompted AI tutor](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6) posted median learning gains more than double those of classmates in an active-learning lesson. All three studies involved teenagers or college students, and none tested young children, so a year's wait for second graders costs little. The high school pilots, which the City says it chose for "keeping students as the primary thinker," look more like the conditions that produced gains than like the open chat window that did harm.

The harder case is the chat window students already have at home. A Pew Research Center [survey of teens nationwide](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/) in fall 2025 found that one in 10 do all or most of their schoolwork with a chatbot's help, and a majority said AI cheating happens at least somewhat often at their school. The department's March draft of its AI guidance was criticized for dodging that question, and the final version, Chalkbeat [noted](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/02/what-to-know-about-nyc-public-schools-generative-ai-ban-screen-time-limits/), seems to add nothing on it. Samuels said schools would need to enlist parents.

Detection software is the tool many teachers have reached for, and the evidence against it is specific. In a [2023 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10382961/), Weixin Liang and colleagues at Stanford ran popular detectors on essays by American eighth-graders and by non-native English writers. The detectors were nearly perfect on the eighth-graders and labeled more than half of the non-native writers' essays as machine-made. The authors "strongly caution against the use of GPT detectors in evaluative or educational settings, particularly when assessing the work of non-native English speakers." They tested 2023 versions of the tools on small samples, and newer detectors may do better. But in 2025-26, 15.4% of the city's public school students were [English language learners](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/reports/doe-data-at-a-glance), the group whose honest work those detectors most often flagged. More than a third of states already tell schools not to rely on detectors alone, and half of the 10 largest districts discourage them, [Chalkbeat found](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/09/02/state-ai-guidance-schools-skirts-cheating-teachers/).

Paige Wyatt, who taught high school English until she quit last year, told Chalkbeat for a national story on AI cheating that she spent her own money on detection tools and wants "real consequences" for students who cheat. She is right that cheating should cost something. A penalty also needs evidence that holds up when a parent challenges it, and a detector's score is weakest for the students most likely to be flagged. The department's guidance already says AI may not be used for grading or for "other decisions about students." Finding that a student cheated is such a decision. The department should say in writing that a detector's score cannot be the basis for one.

That leaves teachers with the evidence they can produce themselves. A draft written in class, or a five-minute conversation about an essay, shows what a student can do without a chatbot, and a teacher can defend it. Radin already goes further than that. A rule requiring one of the two for every graded take-home essay would cost class time, which is the price Chalkbeat's teachers described, and no one knows yet whether it works. "There's no evidence-based policy," said Justin Reich of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Christopher Harris, who directs the school library system for a regional education agency in western New York, made Stevens' point in other words: "The only control they have is their agency in developing assignments."

The department can put the essay rule in its high school guidance this year and post the policies East Side and Brooklyn Collaborative wrote as examples of schools that wrote rules before the City did, though neither requires in-class work. When the coalition reports in April 2027, it should be able to say how many high schools adopted the rule and what their teachers saw.
