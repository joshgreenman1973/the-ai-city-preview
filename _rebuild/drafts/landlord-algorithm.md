In August and September 2024, [auditors from the New York State Comptroller's office](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf) called 311 a dozen times. Each time, they asked to file a complaint about an employer's use of an automated hiring tool. Three of the calls were transferred to the City's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP), the agency that enforces New York City's law on those tools. Eight times, 311 operators sent them to the state Department of Labor. Once, they were told to take it up with the employer.

Over the two years the auditors examined, DCWP [received two complaints](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools) about automated hiring tools. It did not check whether its intake process worked.

The city and state now have rules for software that sorts job applicants and recommends rents. For the hiring law, enforcement mostly waits for complaints, and the people these systems judge almost never see the software. In housing, a newspaper investigation and two lawsuits brought the problems to light. The City should test the tools itself. A yearly review of what employers are required to post, run by DCWP under the law already on the books and paid for in its budget, would catch what complaints miss.

## The hiring law

[Local Law 144 of 2021](https://intro.nyc/1894-2020) requires employers that use an automated employment decision tool to have it audited for bias no more than a year before use, post a summary of the results and tell candidates the tool is being used. DCWP can fine violators [$500 to $1,500 a day](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf). Enforcement began [July 5, 2023](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/about/automated-employment-decision-tools.page). Researchers at Cornell and Data & Society called New York City ["the first jurisdiction globally to mandate bias audits for commercial algorithmic systems."](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01399)

The same researchers ran an early test of the law. With 155 student investigators, they checked 391 employers and found 18 that had posted audit reports and 13 that had posted notices to applicants. They could not call the rest violators. The law lets each employer decide whether its software counts as a covered tool, so a missing audit might be a violation or might be a judgment call. The authors named this ["null compliance."](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01399)

The state comptroller's audit, released [Dec. 2, 2025](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools), found that DCWP gave two attorneys primary responsibility for the law. The agency reviewed 32 companies (22 employers and 10 vendors) flagged in publications by Cornell and the American Civil Liberties Union and found one problem. State auditors went over the 22 employers in that group, using only public information, and found at least 17 instances of potential noncompliance. DCWP had an agreement with the City's Office of Technology and Innovation (OTI) for technical help, including an enforcement workbook, and did not use the workbook. Officials told the auditors that periodically reviewing posted audits could be useful but was not ["worth the resource commitment."](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf)

A search of the Council's bill titles finds no bill to amend Local Law 144 since it passed. Council members have kept proposing rules for private pricing algorithms, among them a bill introduced in May to ban ["surveillance pricing,"](https://intro.nyc/0891-2026) which was laid over in committee in June.

## The housing cases

[ProPublica's Heather Vogell](https://www.propublica.org/article/yieldstar-rent-increase-realpage-rent) reported in October 2022 that RealPage's software recommended rents using a "trove of data RealPage gathers from clients, including private information on what nearby competitors charge." The Justice Department [sued in August 2024](https://www.justice.gov/atr/case/us-and-plaintiff-states-v-realpage-inc). Under a settlement filed in November 2025, RealPage may not use competitors' nonpublic data to set prices, with limited exceptions. It may train its models only on data at least 12 months old and not from active leases, and it must accept a monitor, [chosen by the Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/atr/media/1419471/dl), for three years. The court [entered the judgment](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712.52.0.pdf) on May 19, 2026. It takes effect Nov. 15, and RealPage agreed to follow it in the meantime.

Tenant screening went the same way. Mary Louis and other plaintiffs sued SafeRent Solutions in federal court in Massachusetts in May 2022, [alleging](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.mad.245090/gov.uscourts.mad.245090.132.0.pdf) that its tenant score leaned heavily on debts unrelated to rent and disproportionately rejected applicants who paid with housing vouchers, especially Black and Hispanic applicants. Judge Angel Kelley [approved a settlement](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/63335697/louis-v-saferent-solutions-llc/) in November 2024. SafeRent agreed to pay $1,175,000 into a settlement fund and up to $1.1 million more in legal fees and costs. It also agreed to stop putting a score or an accept/decline recommendation in reports for its "Affordable Model" unless a score has been validated for voucher holders. New York City's own screening rule, the [Fair Chance for Housing Act](https://intro.nyc/0632-2022), bars landlords from looking at a criminal record until they have judged an applicant's other qualifications.

New York state then passed its own law on rent-setting software, General Business Law § 340-b, enacted Oct. 16, 2025. Section 2 bars anyone from helping landlords agree not to compete, including through software that pools their data. Section 3 bars landlords from setting rents based on that software's recommendations. RealPage sued in November 2025 on First Amendment grounds, and the state agreed not to enforce the law while the court considered the company's motion. On Sept. 29, Judge Valerie Caproni blocked enforcement of Section 3 and left Section 2 in force. She [called](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712.52.0.pdf) RealPage's likely success on the merits "a close call."

RealPage says that since the settlement it prices from its customers' own data and public information, with limited exceptions. The judge wrote that, as the state has pleaded it so far, the law's basis "has relatively thin support when considered in the context of RealPage's current product." She told the state to be ready to explain the harm of recommendations built almost entirely on public data. Attorney General Letitia James [moved for reconsideration](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712.56.0.pdf) on Oct. 13. However that motion comes out, the court wants evidence about how the software works now. The state would be better placed to supply it if someone in government had tested the product before the suit began.

## The case for complaints

DCWP officials told the auditors that it is hard to spot noncompliance when an employer takes no visible steps toward complying, and that education plus complaint-driven enforcement works best. The agency handles complaints at scale. In 2024 it received 29,468 across all its work, collected $15.2 million in penalties and [won $12.1 million in restitution](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf). DCWP also suggested that the low count may reflect little interest among New Yorkers or the law's deterrent effect.

In its formal response to the audit, DCWP said the two attorneys carry heavy workloads in other areas and that it received no additional resources to enforce Local Law 144. It said OTI's workbooks "are helpful on complex cases, but are not necessary to use in all circumstances." Proactive reviews take staff time and technical skill that a consumer agency may not have. And the RealPage ruling shows that a broad rule can be blocked in court.

Cost is a fair objection, and it argues for a modest program. The state auditors ran a modest one. Using only public information, they found at least 17 potential violations among the same 22 employers in which DCWP had found one. A job applicant has no way to know whether an employer's audit is missing or out of date, but the City can look on the employer's website, as the auditors did.

## What the City should do

DCWP generally agreed with the comptroller's recommendations, which include fixing how 311 routes these complaints and finding violations ["through research and other leads in addition to complaints."](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf) The City should make that a routine with a public scorecard.

Start with 311. A caller who says "hiring algorithm" should reach DCWP, and the City should test the script every quarter, the way the auditors did. (DCWP says 311 has taken online complaints about these tools since May 2025 and that operators got refresher training in July 2025; the auditors tested phone calls.)

Then run a yearly sweep. Each year, DCWP should pull a fixed sample of large employers in the city and the vendors that sell them screening software, check their posted audits and notices against OTI's workbook and publish the results: companies reviewed, problems found, cases opened, fines collected. The workbook may not be needed in every case, but using it on every company in the sample is what lets one year be compared with the next. DCWP also says it has no staff to spare for this work. The City should take the agency at its word and pay for the sweep in DCWP's budget, so that it does not fall to two attorneys with other caseloads.

Finally, use the sweep to settle the definitional fight that produced null compliance. When an employer says its tool is not covered, DCWP should ask for the basis in writing. A year of those answers would show the Council whether the law's definition of a covered tool needs tightening.
