In August and September 2024, [auditors from the state comptroller's office](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf) called 311 a dozen times and asked how to complain about an employer's use of an automated hiring tool. Only three calls were transferred to the City's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP), the agency that enforces New York City's law on those tools. Most went to the state Department of Labor. One caller was told to take it up with the employer.

Over the two years the auditors examined, DCWP [received two complaints](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools) about these tools and did not check whether its intake worked. When the auditors told the agency to go looking for violations on its own, it refused to put more staff or money into the search. It said it had seen ["no evidence of widespread non-compliance"](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf), in reply to an audit that had just found at least 17 possible violations among 22 employers DCWP had reviewed. The City should pay for a yearly sweep of what employers are required to post, and the Council should write that sweep into the law so it no longer depends on the agency's appetite for it.

## What the auditors found

[Local Law 144 of 2021](https://intro.nyc/1894-2020) requires employers that use an automated employment decision tool to have it audited for bias within a year before use, post a summary of the results and tell candidates the tool is in use. Enforcement began [July 5, 2023](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/about/automated-employment-decision-tools.page). Researchers at Cornell and Data & Society called New York City ["the first jurisdiction globally to mandate bias audits for commercial algorithmic systems."](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01399)

Those researchers also ran an early check. Of 391 employers they examined, 18 had posted audit reports. They could not call the rest violators, because the law lets each employer decide whether its software counts as a covered tool. A missing audit might be a violation or a judgment call, a condition the authors named ["null compliance."](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01399)

The comptroller's audit, released [Dec. 2, 2025](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools), describes a thin operation. DCWP gave two attorneys primary responsibility for the law. It reviewed the websites of companies flagged by Cornell and the American Civil Liberties Union and found one problem. The auditors went over the 22 employers in that group, using nothing but public information, and found at least 17. DCWP had an agreement with the City's Office of Technology and Innovation (OTI) for technical help, including an enforcement workbook for checking audits, and did not use the workbook. Officials told the auditors that periodically reviewing posted audits could be useful but was not ["worth the resource commitment."](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf)

## The rent cases

Rent-setting software shows what happens when no regulator looks until a lawsuit forces the question. [ProPublica reported](https://www.propublica.org/article/yieldstar-rent-increase-realpage-rent) in October 2022 that RealPage recommended rents using a "trove of data RealPage gathers from clients, including private information on what nearby competitors charge." The Justice Department [sued in August 2024](https://www.justice.gov/atr/case/us-and-plaintiff-states-v-realpage-inc), and RealPage settled. A federal judge in North Carolina [entered the final judgment](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.ncmd.99417/gov.uscourts.ncmd.99417.194.0.pdf) on May 19, 2026. It bars RealPage from pricing with competitors' nonpublic data, with limited exceptions, and requires a monitor, [chosen by the Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/atr/media/1419471/dl), for three years. Tenant screening produced a similar case. Voucher holders [sued SafeRent Solutions](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.mad.245090/gov.uscourts.mad.245090.132.0.pdf) in Massachusetts, alleging that its tenant score disproportionately screened them out, and in 2024 the company [settled](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/63335697/louis-v-saferent-solutions-llc/), agreeing to drop the score from its "Affordable Model" reports unless one is validated for voucher holders.

New York state then passed its own law, General Business Law § 340-b, signed Oct. 16, 2025. One section bars anyone from helping landlords agree not to compete, including through software that pools their data. Another bars landlords from setting rents on that software's recommendations. RealPage sued on First Amendment grounds. On Sept. 29, Judge Valerie Caproni preliminarily blocked the landlord provision and left the other in force, calling RealPage's likely success on the merits ["a close call."](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712.52.0.pdf)

RealPage says that since the settlement it prices from each client's own data and public information. The judge wrote that, as the state had pleaded it, the law's basis "has relatively thin support when considered in the context of RealPage's current product," and told the state to be ready to explain the harm of recommendations built almost entirely on public data. Attorney General Letitia James [moved for reconsideration](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712/gov.uscourts.nysd.653712.56.0.pdf) on Oct. 13. Whatever happens to that motion, the court wants evidence about how the software works now, and the state would be better placed to supply it if someone in government had tested the product before the suit began. Nor does the federal judgment crowd out state rules. It says nothing in it limits the power of a state or local government to impose additional requirements on the use of nonpublic data.

## What DCWP refused

The comptroller's report says DCWP officials "generally agreed" with its recommendations. The agency's own response, printed at the end of the report, is more guarded. DCWP accepted fixes to complaint intake, training and 311 routing. It [rejected](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2026-24n6.pdf) the recommendation to study why so few complaints arrive, saying it did not agree that the low number needed addressing. It rejected the recommendation to find violations "through research and other leads in addition to complaints" to the extent that meant committing more resources. It agreed to monitor yearly audits only at businesses it had already dealt with, and it said it might review some of the possible violations the auditors found but would not commit to all of them.

DCWP's attorneys carry other caseloads, the agency got no new money for the law and it enforces dozens of others, taking in nearly 30,000 complaints across all its work in 2024. It also argued that because hiring practices are rarely visible, there is no efficient way to find the employers using these tools. That same invisibility is why complaints don't come. An applicant cannot report software she never knew screened her, a point the comptroller made in reply. An agency that waits for complaints about tools no one can see will find little, and the little it finds becomes its reason not to look.

DCWP's objection is cost, so the mayor and the Council should pay it. The agency's budget should carry enough staff to pull a fixed sample of large employers, and the vendors that sell them screening software, each year; check their posted audits and notices against OTI's workbook; and publish what they find: companies reviewed, problems found, cases opened, fines collected. Using the same workbook on every company in the sample is what lets one year be compared with the next. The 311 fix DCWP accepted should be tested by phone every quarter, the way the auditors tested it.

Because DCWP has said it will not commit more resources to this work, the Council should amend Local Law 144 to require the sweep and its report. A [search of the Council's bill titles](https://intro.nyc/1894-2020) finds no amendment proposed since the law passed, even as members take up rules for other pricing software, among them a [ban on "surveillance pricing"](https://intro.nyc/0891-2026) laid over in committee in June. The amendment should also take on null compliance. An employer that says its tool is not covered should have to say so in writing, with its reasons, and a year of those answers would show the Council whether the definition of a covered tool needs tightening.
