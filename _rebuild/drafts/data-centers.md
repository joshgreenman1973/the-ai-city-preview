In April, the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO), which runs the state's power grid, published its annual book of forecasts, known as the Gold Book. One table tracks "large loads": data centers, chip plants and other big new customers connecting to the high-voltage transmission system. The column for Zone J, which is New York City, [reads zero in every year through 2041](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/20142/2226333/2026-Gold-Book-Public.pdf/a8fd42fe-5a8c-88cb-5052-f93dfd6423b8?t=1777471793334).

Statewide, the line to connect is long. By May 1, [51 projects were asking for nearly 12,670 megawatts](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends), and the grid operator's forecasters expect roughly 2,880 megawatts of that to be running by 2040. Not one project on the Gold Book's list is in the five boroughs.

So for New York City, the AI power boom is a question of who pays. The fights over where the buildings go belong to towns upstate. Yet the Council's one data center bill, [Int. 3](https://intro.nyc/0003-2026), introduced by Speaker Julie Menin in January, would have the City's Office of Long-Term Planning and Sustainability identify sites for data centers here and recommend changes to law or zoning "to facilitate the building of data centers." The Council should not pass it in that form. It should drop the site search and point the study at what the projects coming elsewhere in the state will cost Con Edison customers.

## A grid that is already short

The city's own power problem has other causes. NYISO's short-term studies found the city's margins [deficient as early as this summer](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends) and concluded that old peaker units at Gowanus and Narrows, which wanted to retire, have to keep running. The Champlain Hudson Power Express, a transmission line from Quebec, began delivering energy in May, but the grid operator found that it alone does not close the gap over the longer term.

Con Edison sees the same squeeze. Its [July update](https://documents.dps.ny.gov/public/Common/ViewDoc.aspx?DocRefId=%7B406F719F-0000-C356-9276-8BD2CB4C8457%7D&DocTitle=CECONY+July+2026+Reliability+Needs+Update) on the city's reliability needs projects a transmission shortfall beginning in 2033 and reaching 675 megawatts by 2036. The utility blames economic growth, the electrification of buildings and vehicles under state climate law and the retirement of old generators. The words "data center" appear nowhere in that report or in the [January version](https://www.coned.com/-/media/files/coned/documents/business-partners/business-opportunities/non-emitting-reliability-solutions/reliability-needs-report.pdf). Its [contingency plan](https://documents.dps.ny.gov/public/Common/ViewDoc.aspx?DocRefId=%7BB069719F-0000-C151-ADF1-09DCC3314B92%7D&DocTitle=CECONY+Reliability+Contingency+Plan), filed in July, leans on batteries, and more than 1,000 megawatts of grid-scale storage is already waiting to connect in the city.

That is the grid Int. 3 would have planners scout for data center sites. NYISO calls the city a "dense load pocket," where bringing in enough power on the hottest afternoons is hard. A large campus here would draw on the same scarce capacity that the peakers and the batteries exist to supply. The peakers carry a cost of their own: during the June 2025 heat wave, old gas turbines produced about 2.5% of the energy from city power plants and 28% of their nitrogen oxide emissions. Every megawatt a new campus took from the pocket would make them harder to retire.

The Gold Book's zero has limits. NYISO's queue catches only loads above set sizes, such as more than 10 megawatts at high voltage. [Smaller projects go through the utility](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/20142/2226333/2026-Gold-Book-Public.pdf/a8fd42fe-5a8c-88cb-5052-f93dfd6423b8?t=1777471793334) and are folded into Con Edison's ordinary forecast, so new servers in existing buildings would not show up. What the zero rules out is a big campus in the queue or the forecast.

## Albany moves first

Gov. Kathy Hochul signed [Executive Order 62](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops) on July 14. It puts on hold state environmental permit applications for new or expanded data centers that use 50 megawatts or more until the Department of Public Service finishes an environmental impact statement, a review the governor's office says [will take up to a year](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/first-statewide-moratorium-new-hyperscale-data-centers-launched-governor-kathy-hochul). Facilities used mainly for manufacturing, research, education or medical care are exempt, the state's Empire AI consortium among them, and local permits are untouched.

The Legislature wanted a lower bar. In June both houses passed the [Responsible Data Center Development Act](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-legislature-passes-data-center-moratorium-bill-awaits-governors-approval), which would pause permits for data centers of 20 megawatts or more. By mid-October, the [Senate's record](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A11560) showed it still had not been sent to the governor.

The order's preamble counts "nearly 12 gigawatts" of data center requests in the queue. NYISO describes the same queue as also holding [chip fabrication, advanced manufacturing and hydrogen production](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends), and Micron's two planned chip plants alone account for more than 1,000 megawatts of it, so the governor's figure counts more than data centers.

## What city customers pay

New Yorkers already pay a lot for power. In July the average residential price in the state was [29.9 cents per kilowatt-hour](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_a), against 18.3 cents nationally. In PJM, a neighboring grid market, the price set in the capacity auction, which pays power plants to be ready when needed, rose more than ninefold in one year. [Researchers at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the Brattle Group](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/presentation_retail_price_trends_drivers.pdf) list data center growth among the causes.

Big new customers can also lower rates. The same researchers found that from 2019 to 2024, states where demand grew fastest generally saw overall retail prices fall after inflation, because the fixed cost of the grid was spread over more sales. For households the effect was weaker and [lost statistical significance](https://www.publicpower.org/periodical/article/state-level-load-growth-has-reduced-average-retail-electricity-prices-report), and the researchers warned that it may not hold where supply is tight. By NYISO's own studies, New York City's supply is already short.

City customers share one grid with the towns that would host the campuses. NYISO warns that if large loads arrive faster than new supply, the system will lean on expensive resources and emergency actions, ["placing upward pressure on electricity costs statewide."](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends) Hochul's order says upgrade costs for large loads "should not be paid for by every-day New Yorkers," and the Public Service Commission is working out connection rules for large loads in [Case 26-E-0045](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops).

City voters are wary anyway. In a [Siena poll](https://sri.siena.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/SNY-September-2026-Poll-Crosstabs-9-23-26.pdf) taken in September, likely voters in New York City opposed building large data centers in their area by 62% to 23%, close to the upstate margin, though no large project is queued here.

Int. 3 already asks the planning office to weigh how data centers would affect the grid and utility rates, but only data centers built in the city, which the grid operator does not expect. The Council should strike the site search and the zoning recommendations and keep the rate question, aimed at the projects that are coming. Have the office estimate how much of the cost of serving large loads elsewhere in the state, from new supply to transmission upgrades, could land on Con Edison bills, and file that estimate with the commission in Case 26-E-0045. The governor has promised that ordinary New Yorkers will not pay for those upgrades. A City estimate would tell regulators what keeping that promise means for a Con Edison customer.

Then add a yearly report on the city's own shortfall: how many megawatts of storage and transmission are in service in the city, set against the 675 megawatts Con Edison says it will need by 2036.
