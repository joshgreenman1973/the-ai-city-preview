In April, the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO), which runs the state's power grid, published its annual book of forecasts, known as the Gold Book. One table tracks "large loads": data centers, chip plants and other big new customers connecting to the high-voltage transmission system. The column for Zone J, which is New York City, [reads zero in every year through 2041](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/20142/2226333/2026-Gold-Book-Public.pdf/a8fd42fe-5a8c-88cb-5052-f93dfd6423b8?t=1777471793334).

The statewide queue has grown fast. In 2022, six large-load projects asked to connect, for a total of 1,045 megawatts. By May 1 of this year, [51 projects were asking for nearly 12,670](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends). NYISO's forecasters expect as much as roughly 2,880 megawatts of that to be on the system by 2040. Of the 48 projects on the Gold Book's list, which is current as of March 15, none is in the five boroughs. The biggest single request is a 1,935-megawatt project called St. Lawrence Infrastructure 2.

So for New York City, the AI power boom is a question of who pays. Where the buildings go is an upstate fight. The city does have a grid shortfall of its own, but its utility traces it to growth, electrification and the retirement of old power plants, and the fix it proposes leans on batteries. The Council's one data center bill starts at the other end. It asks where in the city data centers could go.

## The city's own shortfall

Con Edison updates its estimate of the city's reliability needs every six months. Its [July update](https://documents.dps.ny.gov/public/Common/ViewDoc.aspx?DocRefId=%7B406F719F-0000-C356-9276-8BD2CB4C8457%7D&DocTitle=CECONY+July+2026+Reliability+Needs+Update) projects a transmission shortfall of 125 megawatts in 2033, growing to 675 megawatts by 2036 and 2,275 by 2045. The utility blames economic growth, the electrification of buildings and vehicles under state climate law, the retirement of old generators and emissions limits on the ones that remain. Neither that report nor the [January version](https://www.coned.com/-/media/files/coned/documents/business-partners/business-opportunities/non-emitting-reliability-solutions/reliability-needs-report.pdf) contains the words "data center."

NYISO's short-term studies are more urgent. They found the city's margins [deficient as early as this summer](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends) and concluded that peaker units at Gowanus and Narrows, which wanted to retire, have to keep running. The Champlain Hudson Power Express, a transmission line from Quebec, began delivering energy in May, but NYISO found that energizing it alone does not close the city's gap over the longer term.

During the June 2025 heat wave, old gas turbines supplied about 2.5% of the energy from city power plants and 28% of the nitrogen oxide emissions. Replacing them means getting power into what NYISO calls a "dense load pocket" on the hottest afternoons. Con Edison's [contingency plan](https://documents.dps.ny.gov/public/Common/ViewDoc.aspx?DocRefId=%7BB069719F-0000-C151-ADF1-09DCC3314B92%7D&DocTitle=CECONY+Reliability+Contingency+Plan), filed in July, leans on batteries. More than 1,000 megawatts of grid-scale storage already sit in the queue for Zone J, and developers have applied to connect more than 2,700 megawatts of smaller storage to Con Edison's distribution system.

The zero in the Gold Book has limits. NYISO's queue catches only loads above certain sizes: more than 10 megawatts at high voltage, or 80 megawatts or more at lower voltage. [Smaller projects go through the utility](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/20142/2226333/2026-Gold-Book-Public.pdf/a8fd42fe-5a8c-88cb-5052-f93dfd6423b8?t=1777471793334) and are folded into Con Edison's ordinary new-business forecast. So the zero means no large campus is in the queue or the forecast for the city. New servers in existing buildings would not show up in it.

## What Albany did

Gov. Kathy Hochul signed [Executive Order 62](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops) on July 14. It tells the Department of Environmental Conservation to hold in abeyance any discretionary permit application to build or expand a data center that it had not already deemed complete, until the Department of Public Service finishes a generic environmental impact statement. The governor's office says that review [will take up to a year](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/first-statewide-moratorium-new-hyperscale-data-centers-launched-governor-kathy-hochul). The order covers facilities that use or can use 50 megawatts or more. It exempts those used mainly for manufacturing, research, education or medical care, naming the state's Empire AI consortium, and it leaves local permits alone.

The Legislature had gone further. On June 4, both houses passed the [Responsible Data Center Development Act](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-legislature-passes-data-center-moratorium-bill-awaits-governors-approval), which would pause permits for data centers of 20 megawatts or more. As of Oct. 19, the [Senate's record](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A11560) shows the bill has not been delivered to the governor. Empire State Development has since released the [community investment framework](https://esd.ny.gov/communityinvestmentframework) the order called for, which sets a voluntary benchmark of $1 million per megawatt for host communities.

The order's preamble counts "nearly 12 gigawatts" of data center load requests in the NYISO queue. NYISO describes the same queue as including [semiconductor fabrication, advanced manufacturing and hydrogen production](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends). Micron's two chip plants alone account for more than 1,000 megawatts of it.

## The bills

New Yorkers pay a lot for power. In July, the average residential price in the state was [29.9 cents per kilowatt-hour](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_a), against 18.3 cents nationally. Elsewhere, data centers have been tied to higher prices. In PJM, a neighboring grid market, the capacity auction price jumped from $29 to $270 per megawatt-day in one year, and [researchers at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the Brattle Group](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/presentation_retail_price_trends_drivers.pdf) list data center load growth among the causes.

New York runs a separate market. And across states, the same team found that from 2019 to 2024, states with the fastest load growth generally saw overall retail prices fall after inflation, because fixed grid costs were spread over more sales. The relationship was smaller and [lost statistical significance for residential prices alone](https://www.publicpower.org/periodical/article/state-level-load-growth-has-reduced-average-retail-electricity-prices-report), and the researchers warned that it may not hold where supply is tight.

The state's own warnings about data centers and New York bills look ahead. Hochul said development ["threatens to hike up utility bills."](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/first-statewide-moratorium-new-hyperscale-data-centers-launched-governor-kathy-hochul) The executive order warns of grid investments made "in anticipation of loads that may not fully materialize."

## The statewide stake

The best argument for city residents to care about upstate data centers is that they share one grid with the towns that would host them. NYISO warns that if large loads arrive faster than supply, the system may rely more on expensive resources and emergency actions, ["placing upward pressure on electricity costs statewide."](https://www.nyiso.com/documents/d/guest/2026-power-trends) The executive order says data center growth "will require the procurement of additional energy supply." Regional tightness already touches the city's numbers, too. Con Edison's January report says a 175-megawatt cut in capacity sales from PJM increased the city's reliability need, though it does not say why those sales fell.

City voters seem to feel the stake. In a [Siena poll](https://sri.siena.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/SNY-September-2026-Poll-Crosstabs-9-23-26.pdf) taken Sept. 11 to 17, likely voters in New York City opposed building large data centers in their area after the moratorium by 62% to 23%, close to the upstate margin of 66% to 23%, even though no large project is in the queue for the city.

Each of those arguments is about cost. City residents have a large stake in what data centers add to the statewide grid's bills and little in where the buildings go.

## What the Council could change

[Int. 3](https://intro.nyc/0003-2026), introduced in January by Speaker Julie Menin, would have the Office of Long-Term Planning and Sustainability study the feasibility and impact of siting data centers in the city, including their effects on the grid and utility rates, identify potential sites and recommend changes to local law or zoning "to facilitate the building of data centers." It has sat in committee since.

The bill already asks how data centers would affect rates, but only data centers built in the city, and the Gold Book forecasts no large ones here. The Council could amend it to point the same office at the projects that are coming. Strike the site search. Have the office estimate how much of the statewide cost of serving large loads elsewhere in the state, from new supply to transmission upgrades, could land on Con Edison bills. Then have it file that estimate with the Public Service Commission in [Case 26-E-0045](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops), the commission's proceeding on interconnection reforms for large loads. The governor's order already declares that upgrade costs for large loads "should not be paid for by every-day New Yorkers." A City estimate would show regulators what that promise is worth to Con Edison customers.

Last, add a yearly report on the city's own shortfall: how many megawatts of storage and transmission are in service in Zone J, set against Con Edison's projected need of 125 megawatts in 2033 and 675 megawatts by 2036.
