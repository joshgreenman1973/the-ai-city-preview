In June 2023, New York City police officers answered 940 alerts from ShotSpotter, the acoustic gunshot-detection system the New York Police Department has paid for since 2014. They found a confirmed shooting at 122 of them. Officers spent more than 400 hours that month on alerts that were never confirmed as shootings.

Over the same half-year, by the measure written into the City's contract, ShotSpotter beat its performance target in every borough. Both findings come from a [2024 audit by the City comptroller's office](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system). The standard, added when the contract was renewed in 2021, asks whether the system detects and locates 90% of outdoor gunfire within 25 meters. An alert that sends officers to a quiet block does not count against it. The company, now called SoundThinking, reported [94% average monthly accuracy](https://ir.soundthinking.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/305/soundthinking-announces-a-shotspotter-three-year---renewal) for 2024 under that standard.

The current contract expires in December 2027. The City should not renew it unless the new one measures what officers find when they arrive: a monthly count of alerts that led to a confirmed shooting, and a standard the vendor has to meet. The NYPD [told the auditors](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system) that confirmed shootings are the wrong yardstick and that ShotSpotter gets officers to scenes faster, and the audit did find that in June 2023, responses to alerts beat responses to 911 calls about outdoor gunfire by about a minute and a half on average. Faster arrival is worth something. The department has not shown how much, and when the audit asked it to evaluate the system before the last renewal, it said that was not feasible and renewed.

At a Council hearing on Oct. 5, the City's Office of Technology and Innovation, known as OTI, [said](https://nyc.legistar.com/View.ashx?M=F&ID=16072121&GUID=B2DB4127-3040-4B2C-9BDC-5DE47D30860D) it had sent agency lawyers an updated rider for artificial intelligence, or AI, this summer: standard terms for software contracts covering intellectual property, security, use of City data and privacy. Its lawyers recommend attaching the rider even when no AI is planned. But the City has not published it, agencies are not required to use it and the topics OTI listed leave out accuracy testing, error reporting and the right to leave when a system falls short. The City should add those terms, publish the rider and require it in every software contract and every renewal.

## How a pilot became four contracts

ShotSpotter arrived as a "demonstration project," the procurement category City rules reserve for a [short-term pilot](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/overview) of a technology the City does not yet use. [Checkbook NYC](https://www.checkbooknyc.com/) lists the 2014 pilot at $1.5 million. A negotiated contract and two renewals followed, and the four gunshot-detection contracts now total about $76.5 million. SoundThinking also holds a separate sole-source NYPD contract, worth $46.1 million, for its CaseBuilder software.

The audit urged the NYPD not to renew in 2024 without a fuller evaluation, and the department [disagreed](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system). In February 2025, then-Comptroller Brad Lander [declined to register](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/newsroom/comptroller-lander-declines-to-register-shotspotter-contract-renewal) the renewal over procedural flaws, among them an extra one-year option that procurement rules did not allow. His office said ShotSpotter had tried to add the option; the company [said](https://www.soundthinking.com/blog/soundthinking-statement-regarding-comptroller-brad-landers-press-release-on-nypds-shotspotter-contract-registration) the NYPD had asked for it.

The audit played no part in Lander's decision, and under the [City Charter](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/overview) it could not have. The comptroller can hold up a contract over missing money, a missing certification that procurement rules were followed, a barred vendor or suspected corruption, and weak performance terms are not on that list. Checkbook shows the renewal registered eight days later.

## What the contracts leave out

Most of the City's algorithms come from outside vendors. In the 2025 [Local Law 35 inventory](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=1000), 60 of the 86 tools agencies reported involved one. The contract decides what the vendor has to measure, who owns the data and whether the City can leave. In 2017, as the NYPD moved from Palantir's analytics software to a system it built with IBM, Palantir declined to hand over a readable version of the analytic data it had produced, citing its intellectual property, according to a [Brennan Center analysis](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/palantir-contract-dispute-exposes-nypds-lack-transparency) of BuzzFeed's reporting. A Palantir contract with the City's Department of Finance said the company "retains all rights" to its products and barred the City from exporting its "technical data."

The public schools are now sorting out their vendors' AI one contract at a time. Before announcing a one-year [moratorium](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-and-chancellor-samuels-put-students-first-with-nat) on student-facing generative AI in 2-K through eighth grade, the Education Department froze new purchases for nearly two months. Mayor Zohran Mamdani said the department was "disabling" AI features in 38 existing citywide contracts, and a mayoral education adviser said the City would stop using any product whose AI could not be turned off, [Chalkbeat reported](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/02/what-to-know-about-nyc-public-schools-generative-ai-ban-screen-time-limits/). The City has not released a list of the contracts or said whether they give it a right to switch the features off.

Other governments put that right in writing. New York State's technology office, in [standard AI terms](https://ogs.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/04/71022.23420_appendix-c-ai-standard-terms-for-ai-purchases-for-nys.pdf) dated April 2025, requires 90 days' written notice before a vendor adds AI to a product, keeps AI that isn't integral to a system off by default and lets the state terminate "at no cost" if unacceptable AI can't be disabled.

The GovAI Coalition, a group of public agencies San José convened in 2023, publishes a [vendor agreement template](https://www.sanjoseca.gov/home/showpublisheddocument/118649/638774761928370000) for any agency to adopt. It lets the agency order an AI system shut off, offers optional clauses keeping the agency's data its own and exportable at no cost and requires the vendor to give the agency a way to monitor accuracy, including false positive and false negative rates. Under that clause, the NYPD would have received a count of the 818 June 2023 alerts that turned up no confirmed shooting.

Even Washington, under an administration that wants agencies to adopt AI faster, writes these rules. An [April 2025 memo](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-22-Driving-Efficient-Acquisition-of-Artificial-Intelligence-in-Government.pdf) from the Office of Management and Budget tells federal agencies to guard against lock-in through data and model portability and to test systems on their own data, and it bars contracts that would stop agencies from sharing vendors' test results internally. It covers renewals of existing contracts. Standard terms could raise prices or thin out bidders, but vendors that sell to Albany and Washington already sign versions of them.

Nor do the City's existing audit rights reach this far. Its standard [contract appendix](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/doing-business-appendix-a.pdf), in its 2011 version, opens a contractor's books, records and invoices to the comptroller. It gives the City no right to test a model's accuracy on its own data.

## The rider and the 2027 deadline

The City promised contract standards three years ago. Its 2023 [AI Action Plan](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/reports/artificial-intelligence-action-plan.pdf) called for AI contract standards, terms or guidance, which could cover performance, transparency and fairness, within 24 months. OTI's [October 2025 progress report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/AI-Action-Plan-Progress-Report-Year-2.pdf) changed the goal to starting the work in that time. The AI laws the Council passed in 2025 gave a new Office of Algorithmic Accountability the job of setting standards for "developing, [procuring](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6702403&GUID=3022B02C-DBF2-41A8-9EFD-C3B8648E5E27), deploying, and using" AI, written as procedures rather than contract clauses.

That office was due by June. In July, [City & State found](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) no sign of it. On Oct. 5, OTI [testified](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/media/Advocacy-Testimony-Intros-161-T20262602-T20262601-T20262606-T20262605-T20262599-T20262603-504-T20262600.pdf) that it had been "formally established" and that hiring for its six funded positions was underway. At the same [hearing](https://nyc.legistar.com/View.ashx?M=F&ID=16072121&GUID=B2DB4127-3040-4B2C-9BDC-5DE47D30860D), Speaker Julie Menin cited press reports that Jiahao Chen, OTI's director of AI and machine learning, had resigned in frustration over positions held up by the City's budget office. OTI's Sarah Milstein said the City hoped to name the office's director "quite soon." No director had been named by Oct. 19.

The City does not need to wait. OTI, the Law Department and the Mayor's Office of Contract Services could publish the rider and fill it out from the state's text and San José's template: performance measured on the City's own data, with false alarms reported; City ownership of its data and outputs, exportable at no cost; AI features off until an agency turns them on; and the right to end a contract without penalty when a vendor cannot meet those terms. The rider should then be mandatory in every software contract and renewal, the scope OTI already recommends.

The administration announced [30 in-house technologists](https://www.amny.com/politics/mamdani-tech-five-new-pit-crews/) in July, but ShotSpotter's more than 2,000 sensors are part of a system the vendor maintains under its contract. ShotSpotter's contract expires Dec. 12, 2027, and the CaseBuilder contract six weeks earlier. That gives the NYPD a year to start counting what its alerts produce. If the next ShotSpotter contract carries no confirmed-shooting standard, the City should let it lapse.
