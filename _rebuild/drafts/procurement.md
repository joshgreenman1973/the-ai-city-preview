In June 2023, New York City police officers responded to 940 alerts from ShotSpotter, the acoustic gunshot-detection system the New York Police Department has paid for since 2014. They confirmed a shooting at 122 of them, or 13%. Over the first half of that year, by the measure written into the City's contract, ShotSpotter beat its performance target in all five boroughs.

Both findings come from a [2024 audit by the City comptroller's office](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system). The performance standard, added when the contract was renewed in 2021, asked whether the system detected and located 90% of outdoor gunfire within 25 meters. False alarms did not count against it. When the company, now called SoundThinking, announced the next renewal in February 2025, it reported [94% average monthly accuracy](https://ir.soundthinking.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/305/soundthinking-announces-a-shotspotter-three-year---renewal) for 2024, against the contract's 90% standard.

A contract settles what a vendor has to measure and report, who owns the data and whether the City can leave. New York has spent three years building oversight for the algorithms its agencies use: an annual public inventory, an Artificial Intelligence Action Plan and, in late 2025, laws creating an Office of Algorithmic Accountability. Most of that work looks at systems after the City has bought them.

The City's Office of Technology and Innovation, known as OTI, has begun work on the contracts themselves. At a Council hearing on Oct. 5, OTI [said](https://nyc.legistar.com/View.ashx?M=F&ID=16072121&GUID=B2DB4127-3040-4B2C-9BDC-5DE47D30860D) it had sent agency lawyers an updated AI rider this summer: standard terms, available for every City software contract, covering intellectual property, security, how City data may be used and privacy. OTI's legal team recommends that agencies attach it even when no AI is planned.

The City has not published the rider, and agencies are not required to use it. The topics OTI listed did not include testing a system's accuracy, reporting its errors or ending a contract when a system falls short. The City should add those terms, publish the rider and make it mandatory for every software contract and every renewal.

## How a pilot became a $76.5 million contract

In the 2025 [Local Law 35 inventory](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=1000), 60 of the 86 algorithmic tools City agencies reported involved an outside vendor.

According to [Checkbook NYC](https://www.checkbooknyc.com/), ShotSpotter's first contract, in 2014, was a "demonstration project," which City procurement rules define as a [short-term pilot](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/overview) to test a technology the City does not yet use. It was written for $1.5 million. A negotiated contract followed in 2016, then renewals in 2021 and 2024. The four contracts now total about $76.5 million. The current one runs through 2027.

The audit urged the NYPD not to renew in 2024 without a fuller evaluation; the department [disagreed](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system), saying an evaluation before renewal was not feasible. In February 2025, then-Comptroller Brad Lander [declined to register](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/newsroom/comptroller-lander-declines-to-register-shotspotter-contract-renewal) the renewal over procedural flaws, including an extra one-year option that procurement rules did not allow. Lander's office said ShotSpotter had tried to add it; the company [said](https://www.soundthinking.com/blog/soundthinking-statement-regarding-comptroller-brad-landers-press-release-on-nypds-shotspotter-contract-registration) the NYPD had asked for it. His office said the audit was not a factor, because its registration power "only covers procedural requisites." Under the [City Charter](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/overview), the comptroller can hold up a contract over missing funds, a missing certification that procurement rules were followed, a debarred vendor or suspected corruption. Weak performance terms are not on the list.

Checkbook shows the renewal registered eight days later.

## Who owns the data

In 2017, as the NYPD moved from Palantir's analytics software to a system it built with IBM, Palantir declined to hand over a readable version of the analytic data it had produced, citing its intellectual property, according to a [Brennan Center analysis](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/palantir-contract-dispute-exposes-nypds-lack-transparency) of BuzzFeed's reporting. A Palantir contract with the City's Department of Finance said the company "retains all rights" to its products and barred the City from exporting its "technical data." Intellectual property and the use of City data are both on OTI's list of what its rider covers.

## Turning AI off in the schools

New York's public schools are sorting out their vendors' AI one contract at a time. Before announcing a one-year [moratorium](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-and-chancellor-samuels-put-students-first-with-nat) on student-facing generative AI in 2-K through eighth grade, the Education Department froze new purchases for nearly two months, [Chalkbeat reported](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/02/what-to-know-about-nyc-public-schools-generative-ai-ban-screen-time-limits/). Mayor Zohran Mamdani said the department was "disabling" AI features in 38 existing citywide contracts; the City has not released a list. Where a company can't turn its AI features off, the City will stop using the product, said Ailish Brady, the mayor's education advisor. Whether those contracts gave the City a right to switch the features off has not been made public.

The state's technology office puts that right in its contracts. Its [standard AI terms](https://ogs.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/04/71022.23420_appendix-c-ai-standard-terms-for-ai-purchases-for-nys.pdf), dated April 2025, require 90 days' written notice before a vendor adds AI to a product, say AI that isn't integral to a system should be off by default and let the state terminate "at no cost" if unacceptable AI can't be disabled. They also bar vendors from training models on state data without permission.

## Terms other governments use

The GovAI Coalition, a group of public agencies San José convened in November 2023, publishes a [vendor agreement template](https://www.sanjoseca.gov/home/showpublisheddocument/118649/638774761928370000) any agency can adopt. It lets the agency order an AI system shut off, offers optional clauses keeping the agency's data its own and exportable at no cost and requires the vendor to give the agency a way to monitor accuracy, naming false positive and false negative rates. A ShotSpotter contract with that clause could have required a count of the 818 June 2023 alerts that turned up no confirmed shooting.

Washington, under an administration that wants agencies to adopt AI faster, has written similar rules. An [April 2025 memo](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-22-Driving-Efficient-Acquisition-of-Artificial-Intelligence-in-Government.pdf) from the Office of Management and Budget tells federal agencies to build in protections against lock-in, including data and model portability, and to test systems on their own data. Contracts "must not prohibit agencies from internally disclosing" vendors' test results, and the memo covers renewals of existing contracts.

## New York's plan and the new office

The City's 2023 [AI Action Plan](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/reports/artificial-intelligence-action-plan.pdf) called for AI contract standards, terms or guidance, which could cover performance, transparency and fairness, to be completed within 24 months. OTI's [October 2025 progress report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/AI-Action-Plan-Progress-Report-Year-2.pdf) changed that to "initiate" the work within 24 months. A package of AI bills the Council passed in 2025 gave the new algorithmic accountability office the job of setting standards for "developing, [procuring](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6702403&GUID=3022B02C-DBF2-41A8-9EFD-C3B8648E5E27), deploying, and using" AI. Those standards are written as procedures and name no contract clauses.

In July, [City & State found](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) no sign of the office, which was due by June. On Oct. 5, OTI [testified](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/media/Advocacy-Testimony-Intros-161-T20262602-T20262601-T20262606-T20262605-T20262599-T20262603-504-T20262600.pdf) that it had been "formally established" and that hiring for its six funded positions was "actively underway." As of Oct. 19, no director had been named.

## The case against

Standard terms can raise prices or thin out bidders, and the vendors with the most proprietary systems have the most room to refuse. The City also already has audit rights. Its standard [contract appendix](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/doing-business-appendix-a.pdf) lets the comptroller examine a contractor's books, and the ShotSpotter audit shows the office can find problems. And the Mamdani administration is betting partly on building its own tools: in July it announced [30 in-house technologists](https://www.amny.com/politics/mamdani-tech-five-new-pit-crews/) at a cost of $5.24 million.

The NYPD also disputes the audit's yardstick. It [told the auditors](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system) that confirmed shootings should not be used to measure ShotSpotter's performance and that the system speeds up response; the audit found that in June 2023, responses to alerts were on average 1 minute 38 seconds faster than responses to 911 reports of outdoor shots fired. The contract also has a penalty: when the system misses the 90% standard in a borough in two months of a quarter, the City gets a credit equal to two weeks of that borough's fees.

The standard appendix, in its 2011 version, reaches books, records and invoices, which is how auditors check a bill. It gives the City no right to test a model's accuracy on its own data. The comptroller questioned the ShotSpotter measure in 2024, and the NYPD renewed the contract anyway. In-house teams can build software, but the NYPD's more than 2,000 ShotSpotter sensors are maintained by the vendor under its contract. As for price, versions of these terms are already standard for the state's technology office and required of federal agencies, so vendors have seen them before.

## What the City can do

The City does not need to wait for the new office's rules. OTI, the Law Department and the Mayor's Office of Contract Services could publish the AI rider and fill it out from the state's text and San José's template. The rider should spell out four terms: performance measured on the City's own data, with false positives reported; City ownership of its data and outputs, exportable at no cost; AI features off until an agency turns them on; and termination without penalty when a vendor cannot meet those terms. Then the City should make the rider mandatory in every software contract and every renewal, the same scope OTI already recommends.

ShotSpotter's current contract with the NYPD expires Dec. 12, 2027. Before it is renewed, the City can write in a monthly report of how many alerts led to a confirmed shooting.
