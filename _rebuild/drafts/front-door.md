In testing published in March 2024, reporters at The Markup asked New York City's new artificial intelligence chatbot whether landlords had to accept Section 8 vouchers. At one point it told a reporter they did. Then 10 Markup staff members asked the same question, and [the bot told every one of them no](https://themarkup.org/news/2024/03/29/nycs-ai-chatbot-tells-businesses-to-break-the-law). In New York City, refusing a tenant because of how the rent gets paid is illegal, with a narrow exception for small owner-occupied buildings. The same bot told users that a boss could take a cut of workers' tips and that a restaurant could refuse cash. It made similar errors in other languages.

Mayor Eric Adams had [unveiled the bot in October 2023](https://www.nyc.gov/office-of-the-mayor/news/777-23/mayor-adams-releases-first-of-its-kind-plan-responsible-artificial-intelligence-use-nyc) as part of his MyCity portal, promising business owners "trusted information from more than 2,000 NYC Business web pages." After The Markup's story, his administration [added disclaimers, fixed some answers and narrowed what the bot would take on](https://themarkup.org/artificial-intelligence/2026/01/30/mamdani-to-kill-the-nyc-ai-chatbot-we-caught-telling-businesses-to-break-the-law), and Adams promised "the best chatbot system on the globe." In January 2026, facing a $12 billion budget gap, Mayor Zohran Mamdani called it "functionally unusable" and said it was costing around half a million dollars. The City took it down in early February. [Its old address](https://chat.nyc.gov/) now carries a page titled "The Chatbot beta test has ended."

Shutting it down was the right call. In July, a City Hall spokesperson, Jeremy Edwards, told City & State that the administration would use AI ["only where it demonstrably improves life for New Yorkers."](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) That standard needs a measurement, and MyCity never had a public one. Neither the City's annual filings on its algorithmic tools nor the Mayor's Management Report gives an accuracy rate for the bot.

Any tool the City puts in front of residents to answer their questions should have a narrow job and a published accuracy rate, measured before launch and reported every year.

## The pressure on 311's phones

The City's 311 line took 16.6 million calls in fiscal 2026, according to the [Mayor's Management Report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/311.pdf), down from 18.2 million four years earlier. Calls fell as more people used the app and the website. Waits got longer anyway. The line answered 75% of high-volume calls within 30 seconds, short of its 80% target, and the average wait rose to 43 seconds from 25 the year before. The City blamed severe winter weather. Phone service is available in 175 languages, and in fiscal 2026 the line took about 1.7 million calls in Spanish and 260,000 in other languages besides English.

Matthew Fraser, Adams' chief technology officer, [said in March 2025](https://www.cityandstateny.com/personality/2025/03/matt-fraser-still-wants-expand-mycity-and-ai-chatbot/403899/) that more than 70% of calls to 311 are requests for information, and that the City planned to feed all of 311's content into the chatbot and add a voice version. The City's annual disclosure of algorithmic tools under Local Law 35 shows that by 2025 the bot's content [had been expanded to include 311 knowledge articles](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=1000), and that the City classed it as "fully autonomous." Fraser said hallucinations had "gone down exponentially" after a patch that, by his account, turned away questions outside the bot's scope. He gave no figure.

Other City tools built on AI work differently. Since 2024, a Microsoft translation tool has let English-speaking 311 agents answer text messages in the 10 designated citywide languages; the agent writes the reply and the machine translates it. Since December 2025, the 311 app has suggested complaint categories from a photo and let the user choose. The Department of Youth and Community Development has run a chatbot since 2020 that handles about 2,100 requests a day. The City rates it "fully autonomous" too, the same as MyCity. The difference is in the answers: the youth department's bot returns the best match from a knowledge base its staff maintain, while MyCity generated its replies with OpenAI's GPT-4o model.

## Denver's chatbot

Denver has run a city chatbot, Sunny, [since February 2024](https://www.westword.com/news/denver-ai-chatbot-sunny-knows-basics-cant-help-on-hot-topics-20891202). It cost $184,000 in its first year and draws only on the city's website and its 311 knowledge base. By the city's count, it answers in 72 languages, and by mid-2024 it accounted for 12% of 311 inquiries. When Westword tested it, a pothole report went smoothly, but questions about construction, lead in water and events got thin answers and outdated links. Denver's [own page for Sunny](https://denvergov.org/Sunny) warns that its answers "may contain errors, omissions, inaccuracies, and outdated information."

In October 2025, facing a $200 million budget hole, Denver [shortened 311's staffed hours](https://www.denvergazette.com/?p=345381) to 7 a.m. to 5 p.m. on weekdays, eliminated nine vacant agent positions and one filled one and pointed residents to Sunny after hours. On Oct. 13, 2026, Denver's City Council [voted 9-3 against a five-year, $3.9 million contract extension](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/denver-ai-chatbot-sunny-may-go-dark-after-contract-vote) that would also have added a 311 voice bot. Opponents said Sunny often gives inaccurate information and takes jobs people should have. Mayor Mike Johnston's office cited a 90% satisfaction rating and said about 40% of 311 contacts now come through Sunny and the online reporting system. The contract is paid through the end of October. A city technology official told the council the service would not continue without the contract; Johnston's office said it would meet with the council to find a way to keep it running. As of this writing, no deal has been announced.

Both sides in that fight argued without an accuracy figure. Satisfaction measures whether people liked an answer, and a confident wrong answer can please the person who gets it. The council's claim that Sunny is often wrong and the mayor's claim that it works could both have been checked against a graded sample of real questions. No such figure appears in the coverage of the vote or on the city's page for Sunny.

## A government that published its score

Britain's Government Digital Service did publish. Its first experiment with a chatbot grounded in GOV.UK guidance [fell short](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2024/01/18/the-findings-of-our-first-generative-ai-experiment-gov-uk-chat/), and the team said so in public: answers "did not reach the highest level of accuracy demanded," and some users trusted the bot too much because it carried the government's name. The team kept testing. Across two public pilots, more than 10,000 people asked 26,000 questions, and subject experts plus automated checks graded the answers. [Accuracy rose from 76% to 90%](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2026/03/16/5-things-we-learned-testing-gov-uk-chat-an-ai-assistant-for-government/), with many misses being answers that covered only part of a question. The bot draws only on published guidance, answers 88% of questions within its scope and blocked all 508 attempts to trick it. It [went live in the GOV.UK app](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2026/05/14/gov-uk-chat-launches/) this spring. (A disclosure: the current version runs on Claude models made by Anthropic, the company whose model wrote this essay.)

The government graded its own bot, though. A 90% score means about one answer in 10 falls short, and as of March the bot could not hand a user to a human adviser.

## The case against trying again

The strongest objection is that a city-branded bot does its harm out of sight. A wrong answer about a tenant's rights or a worker's tips can cost someone money, and the British team found that some people trust a government bot more than they should. New York's human line already does well by its own measures: 311 scores 92% on its customer satisfaction index. The City's [language access report for fiscal 2025](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/immigrants/downloads/pdf/FY25-Language-Access-Annual-Report.pdf) warns that some agencies already lean on unsupervised machine translation and that accuracy drops for languages such as Wolof and Fulani. And in Denver, when the city needed savings, it trimmed 311's hours and staff and sent after-hours callers to Sunny. The city presented the cut as a budget measure. A skeptic would say the bot made it easier to make.

Those are good arguments for keeping the job narrow and keeping people on the phones. But Fraser put information questions at more than 70% of 311's calls, and a tool that answered them reliably would leave agents more time for callers with harder problems. MyCity got rules about rights and money wrong. When The Markup first tested it, it had no limits on what it would answer, and the City never published an accuracy rate for it. A bot limited to 311's own knowledge articles, kept away from legal advice and graded in public would be a different product.

## A number in the management report

The 311 chapter of the Mayor's Management Report already tracks wait times and satisfaction every year. Before any AI tool answers a resident's question, the Office of Technology and Innovation should build a test set of real 311 information questions in the 10 designated citywide languages, have 311 staff grade the tool's answers against the City's knowledge articles and publish the accuracy rate in that chapter as a performance indicator with a target. A tool that misses its target should stay off. Asking for a human should reach a person at once, as Denver's voice bot proposal [promised](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/people-call-311-denver-help-035324965.html), and the City should not cut 311's staffed hours on the strength of the bot.

The fiscal 2026 report came out in September. The first accuracy rate could appear in the next one, in the same table as 311's call counts and wait times.
