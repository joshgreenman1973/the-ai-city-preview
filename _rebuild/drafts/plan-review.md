New York City's Department of Buildings (DOB) is quick at the step that artificial intelligence (AI) plan-check tools are built to speed up. In the fiscal year that ended in June, its examiners took an average of [5.4 days](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/dob.pdf) to complete a first review of an application filed through DOB NOW, the agency's online portal. The average application took 25.4 days to get from filing to approval.

The department says most of that time belongs to the applicant. According to the [Mayor's Management Report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/dob.pdf), the time a filing spends with the applicant for corrections "is typically three times as long as the time the filing is with the Department for review."

Automated pre-checks scan drawings for missing documents and code violations before an examiner opens them, so they go after exactly those round trips between applicant and examiner. Early numbers from Honolulu are encouraging. But New York's whole DOB approval loop averages under a month, and a building permit needs sign-off from [10-plus City agencies](https://www.vitalcitynyc.org/nyc-housing-permits-fast-track-construction-mamdani/), by the count of Melissa O'Shaughnessy's Just Fix It column in Vital City. A city that buys a pre-check should judge it by the total time to a permit, a number New York does not yet publish.

## Honolulu's test

Honolulu now requires a pre-check. Its Department of Planning and Permitting (DPP) [launched CivCheck](https://www.honolulu.gov/dpp/?p=37889) in December 2025 as "AI-guided permit preparation software" for residential plans. Applicants upload drawings, answer questions about the project and fix what the software flags before they file with the city. Director Dawn Takeuchi Apuna said at the launch that incomplete, poor-quality applications needing extra correction cycles are "one of the biggest challenges that create permitting delays." Since Sept. 1, the tool has been [mandatory](https://www.hawaiinewsnow.com/2026/09/01/new-ai-tool-now-required-some-building-permit-applications-oahu/) for eligible residential projects, and applications that skip it are rejected.

By DPP's count, CivCheck applications averaged [32.5 days of review against 73 days](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/honolulu-launches-ai-assisted-fast-track-permit-review) for a comparison group, and 1.4 review cycles against 3.4. DPP says corrections per permit [fell from 23.5 to 7.7](https://www.honolulu.gov/dpp/?p=42330).

The figures rest on 19 CivCheck permits and 17 comparison permits. "We recognize that 19 completed applications is a relatively small sample," DPP spokesman Davis Pitner told the Honolulu Star-Advertiser. Use was voluntary when the data were collected, so the designers who signed up early may have been the ones with cleaner plans to begin with. In July, DPP also began sending CivCheck filings [straight to the prescreen stage, ahead of other applications](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/honolulu-launches-ai-assisted-fast-track-permit-review), and it has not said whether any of the 19 permits got that treatment. The comparison cannot separate the tool from the people who chose it, or possibly from their place in line. The figures don't fully agree, either: the chief executive of Clariti, the company that built Honolulu's permit system, [told Hawaii News Now](https://www.hawaiinewsnow.com/2026/09/01/new-ai-tool-now-required-some-building-permit-applications-oahu/) that items needing fixes fell from 14 or 15 to three or four.

"If we can improve the quality of the application before it enters the government review, we can eliminate entire cycles of correction," Apuna said, as [quoted by Hawaii Public Radio](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/text/local-news/2026-09-24/honolulu-building-permit-applications-rise-under-ai-processing-tools) in September. "That's potentially much more powerful than asking every reviewer to work a little faster."

The same reasoning applies in New York, where DOB completed 111,842 resubmission reviews in DOB NOW in fiscal 2026, against 156,389 first reviews. The resubmissions are what a pre-check is meant to cut.

Honolulu's citywide numbers look different. Hawaii Public Radio [reported](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/text/local-news/2026-09-24/honolulu-building-permit-applications-rise-under-ai-processing-tools) that residential permits issued in 2026 spent an average of 218 days from application to issuance, and commercial permits 469. Applications have risen 34% since the city's new permitting system launched, while permits issued are up 5%, according to Honolulu City Council member Tyler Dos Santos-Tam. The backlog stands at 9,252, a count DPP says includes dormant files from before the system went digital. The 32.5-day and 218-day figures come from different sets of permits, so one cannot be subtracted from the other. They do show that plan review is one stage of a longer wait. Even CivCheck's fast lane, DPP notes, leaves applications [subject to other agencies](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/honolulu-launches-ai-assisted-fast-track-permit-review), including the Board of Water Supply.

## Los Angeles after the fires

Los Angeles adopted the same idea under more pressure. After the January 2025 fires, Gov. Gavin Newsom [announced](https://statescoop.com/california-ai-pilot-building-permits-la-wildfires/) that Los Angeles city and county would get free use of Archistar, an AI pre-check made by a Sydney company, through a partnership of the state and philanthropies. The city [began a voluntary beta](https://mayor.lacity.gov/news/city-launches-beta-testing-ai-tool-further-expedite-palisades-rebuilding) for Pacific Palisades rebuilds in July 2025, asking early users to submit plans "to help train the AI tool." In the county's version, results [could take up to 10 business days](https://recovery.lacounty.gov/2025/07/15/la-county-launches-echeck-ai-pilot-as-part-of-express-lane-for-faster-rebuilding/). The city's [rebuilding page](https://recovery.lacity.gov/rebuilding) still offers the tool. Neither that page, the county's nor a [January 2026 presentation](https://file.lacounty.gov/SDSInter/dpw/recovery/1201690_RebuildingTogether_ArchistarDRAFTPPT12226.pdf) by the county, the city and Archistar gives a count of users or a measured time savings.

The changes Los Angeles can point to were procedural. The city says it [eliminated 70% of the clearances](https://recovery.lacity.gov/rebuilding) required for single-family rebuild permits and told departments to review complete applications within 30 days. Mayor Karen Bass also created a [self-certification pilot](https://mayor.lacity.gov/news/6-months-start-wildfires-mayor-bass-highlights-recovery-progress-outlines-priorities-continued) that lets licensed architects vouch for code compliance without a full plan check.

A year after the fire, the city's Department of Building and Safety told [Crosstown](https://xtown.la/2026/01/05/post-fire-rebuilding-palisades/), a University of Southern California news site, that Palisades permits were averaging 49 days from application to issuance. One builder said a single house had needed 16 clearances. David P. Waite, a land-use lawyer who worked on an outside rebuilding blueprint, told Crosstown the main hurdle was approvals from more than a dozen departments, including Planning, Engineering and Water and Power, and that coordination "still remains a bottleneck."

## What New York has tried

New York has tested these tools without adopting one. In 2025, DOB and the Partnership Fund for New York City ran a [Buildings Tech Lab](https://buildingstechlab.nyc/news/inaugural-buildings-tech-lab-releases-proof-of-concept-results-revealing-ways-tech-can-reduce-nyc-red-tape) in which eight companies spent eight weeks testing products with the department. CivCheck [pre-screened 14 residential alteration and enlargement plans](https://buildingstechlab.nyc/proof-of-concept-results-2025) against 50 checks, and six DOB plan reviewers reported a 25% time savings after using the tool. Archistar loaded one chapter of the Zoning Resolution and checked three applications against 26 zoning rules. Its claim of two to four fewer review cycles per application was the company's estimate.

The lab's [news page](https://buildingstechlab.nyc/news) has announced nothing since a September 2025 call for a second round. DOB has never filed an entry in the Local Law 35 reports in which City agencies list their algorithmic tools; the [dataset](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=500) holds 218 entries from 2022 through 2025, none from Buildings. O'Shaughnessy's column, published in May, urged Mayor Zohran Mamdani's administration to "aggressively explore" AI for permitting and noted that permitting went unmentioned in the City's 2023 AI Action Plan.

There is a case for moving. DOB's own clock is slipping: first reviews took 3.7 days in fiscal 2025 and 5.4 in fiscal 2026, and filing-to-approval rose from 20.3 days to 25.4. A pre-check that cut even one correction cycle from a large share of filings would show up in those numbers, and the Honolulu and Tech Lab results, thin as they are, point in that direction.

The gain would still be bounded. By DOB's three-to-one rule of thumb, about 19 of the 25.4 days sit with the applicant. Cut that in half and the average filing would get through about 9.5 days sooner. O'Shaughnessy's column put the time for as-of-right projects to collect their permits at one to two years. If that is close, DOB's approval loop is a small part of the total, and the rest passes in stages, including other agencies' reviews, that a tool reading plans for DOB does not reach.

## What DOB could do

If DOB pilots a pre-check, it should assign eligible filings at random, for instance by alternating job numbers, so the results don't depend on which architects volunteer. That is the flaw that limits Honolulu's numbers. It should judge the pilot on what applicants experience: days from filing to approval, the number of resubmissions and days to the final permit, with the comparison group's numbers published next to the pilot's.

The Mayor's Management Report should also print the split DOB already describes in prose. The department says applicants hold filings about three times as long as examiners do. It should publish the applicant's days and the examiner's days separately, by job type, in every annual report.
