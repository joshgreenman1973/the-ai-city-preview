New York City's Department of Buildings (DOB) is quick at the step that artificial intelligence (AI) plan-check tools are built to speed up. In the fiscal year that ended in June, its examiners took an average of [5.4 days](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/dob.pdf) to finish a first review of an application filed through DOB NOW, the agency's online portal. Getting from filing to approval took 25.4 days.

The department says most of that time belongs to the applicant. According to the [Mayor's Management Report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/operations/downloads/pdf/mmr2026/dob.pdf), a filing typically sits with the applicant for corrections about three times as long as it sits with DOB for review, and DOB counts that time as outside its control.

Automated pre-checks scan drawings for missing documents and code violations before an examiner opens them, so they go after exactly those round trips. In fiscal 2026, DOB ran about seven resubmission reviews in DOB NOW for every 10 first reviews. But a building permit needs sign-off from [10-plus City agencies](https://www.vitalcitynyc.org/nyc-housing-permits-fast-track-construction-mamdani/), by the count of Vital City's Just Fix It column on permitting, and a tool that reads plans for DOB can shorten only DOB's part of the wait.

## Honolulu's 19 permits

Honolulu has gone furthest. Its Department of Planning and Permitting (DPP) [launched CivCheck](https://www.honolulu.gov/dpp/?p=37889) in December 2025 to screen residential plans before they are filed. Applicants upload drawings, answer questions about the project and fix what the software flags. Director Dawn Takeuchi Apuna said at the launch that incomplete applications needing extra rounds of correction are one of the biggest causes of permitting delay. Since Sept. 1, the tool has been [mandatory](https://www.hawaiinewsnow.com/2026/09/01/new-ai-tool-now-required-some-building-permit-applications-oahu/) for eligible residential projects, and applications that skip it are rejected.

The published evidence for it is small. By DPP's count, CivCheck applications spent [32.5 days in review against 73](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/honolulu-launches-ai-assisted-fast-track-permit-review) for a comparison group and went through fewer than half as many review cycles. Those figures come from 19 CivCheck permits and 17 others, a sample DPP's spokesman called "relatively small." Use was voluntary when the data were collected, so the designers who signed up early may have been the ones with cleaner plans to begin with. And at the end of June, DPP began sending CivCheck filings [straight to the prescreen stage, ahead of other applications](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/honolulu-launches-ai-assisted-fast-track-permit-review), without saying whether any of the 19 got that treatment. The comparison cannot separate the tool from the people who chose it, or possibly from their place in line.

"If we can improve the quality of the application before it enters the government review, we can eliminate entire cycles of correction," she said, as [quoted by Hawaii Public Radio](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/text/local-news/2026-09-24/honolulu-building-permit-applications-rise-under-ai-processing-tools) in September. "That's potentially much more powerful than asking every reviewer to work a little faster."

The rest of Honolulu's system shows how much of the wait lies elsewhere. Residential permits issued in 2026 took an average of [218 days](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/text/local-news/2026-09-24/honolulu-building-permit-applications-rise-under-ai-processing-tools) from application to issuance, the station reported, and a member of the Honolulu City Council noted that applications have risen far faster than permits issued since the city's new permitting system launched. The 218 days and the 32.5 come from different sets of permits, so one cannot be subtracted from the other. Even CivCheck's fast lane leaves applications [subject to other agencies' reviews](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/honolulu-launches-ai-assisted-fast-track-permit-review), including the Board of Water Supply.

Los Angeles tried the same idea under more pressure. After the January 2025 fires, Gov. Gavin Newsom [announced](https://statescoop.com/california-ai-pilot-building-permits-la-wildfires/) that Los Angeles city and county would get free use of Archistar, an AI pre-check made by a Sydney company. The city [opened a voluntary beta](https://mayor.lacity.gov/news/city-launches-beta-testing-ai-tool-further-expedite-palisades-rebuilding) for Pacific Palisades rebuilds that July, asking early users to submit plans to help train the tool. Its [rebuilding page](https://recovery.lacity.gov/rebuilding) still offers the tool. Neither that page, the county's nor a [January 2026 presentation](https://file.lacounty.gov/SDSInter/dpw/recovery/1201690_RebuildingTogether_ArchistarDRAFTPPT12226.pdf) by the county, the city and Archistar reports how many people used it or what it measurably saved.

What Los Angeles can point to came from cutting steps. The city says it [eliminated 70% of the clearances](https://recovery.lacity.gov/rebuilding) required for single-family rebuild permits, and Mayor Karen Bass created a [self-certification pilot](https://mayor.lacity.gov/news/6-months-start-wildfires-mayor-bass-highlights-recovery-progress-outlines-priorities-continued) that lets licensed architects vouch for code compliance without a full plan check. A year after the fire, Palisades permits were averaging [49 days](https://xtown.la/2026/01/05/post-fire-rebuilding-palisades/) from application to issuance, the city's building department told Crosstown, a University of Southern California news site. David P. Waite, a land-use lawyer who worked on an outside rebuilding blueprint, told the site that the main hurdle was still approvals from more than a dozen departments, among them Planning, Engineering and Water and Power.

## New York's tryout

New York has tested these tools without adopting one. In 2025, DOB and the Partnership Fund for New York City ran a [Buildings Tech Lab](https://buildingstechlab.nyc/news/inaugural-buildings-tech-lab-releases-proof-of-concept-results-revealing-ways-tech-can-reduce-nyc-red-tape) in which eight companies spent eight weeks working with the department. CivCheck [pre-screened 14 residential alteration and enlargement plans](https://buildingstechlab.nyc/proof-of-concept-results-2025), and the DOB plan reviewers who used it reported a 25% time savings. Archistar checked a few applications against one chapter of the Zoning Resolution and estimated, on its own reckoning, that it could cut two to four review cycles per application. The lab's [news page](https://buildingstechlab.nyc/news) has announced nothing since a September 2025 call for a second round, and DOB has never listed a tool in the [Local Law 35 reports](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=500) in which City agencies disclose their algorithms.

The argument for moving faster is DOB's own clock. First reviews took 3.7 days in fiscal 2025 and 5.4 in fiscal 2026, and the time from filing to approval has risen every year since fiscal 2022. Vital City's Just Fix It column, published in May, urged Mayor Zohran Mamdani's administration to "aggressively explore" AI for permitting and noted that the City's 2023 AI Action Plan left permitting out. A pre-check that removed even one correction cycle from many filings would show up in DOB's numbers, and the Honolulu and Tech Lab results, thin as they are, point that way.

The gain would still be modest next to the whole wait. By DOB's three-to-one rule of thumb, about 19 of the 25.4 days sit with the applicant. Halve that and the average filing gets through DOB about 9.5 days sooner. The same column put the time for as-of-right projects to collect their permits at one to two years. If that is close, DOB's approval loop is a small slice of the total, and the rest passes in stages, including other agencies' reviews, that a tool reading plans for DOB does not touch.

So the question for New York is how to try one. Honolulu's mandate arrived with published evidence from 19 permits whose applicants had opted in, and New York can learn far more than that for the price of a coin flip. If DOB tests a pre-check, it should assign eligible filings to it at random, for instance by alternating job numbers, and publish the pilot group's days to approval and count of resubmissions beside the comparison group's before it requires any applicant to use the tool.
