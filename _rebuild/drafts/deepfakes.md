About 20 minutes into the Oct. 22, 2025, mayoral debate, Andrew Cuomo's campaign posted a two-minute video made with artificial intelligence. It opened on an [AI-generated Zohran Mamdani eating rice with his bare hands](https://www.amny.com/politics/cuomo-backlash-ai-generated-ad/), then rolled through a cast of synthetic criminals — a shoplifter in a keffiyeh, a pimp, a domestic abuser, a drunk driver, a drug dealer — all happy to endorse him.

The campaign pulled it. Spokesman Rich Azzopardi called it "a draft proposal that was neither finished nor approved," posted by a junior staffer, "which is why it was taken down five minutes later." The journalist Prem Thakker re-uploaded it on X, where his copy [drew more than 2 million views](https://www.amny.com/politics/cuomo-backlash-ai-generated-ad/).

Common Cause New York told The New York Times that the video may have broken state law by not disclosing its use of AI; [404 Media later reported](https://www.404media.co/bruce-blakeman-ai-campaign-video-mamdani-hochul/) that it was labeled as AI. No lawsuit over it surfaced in press coverage. Cuomo kept at it, with [an AI Cuomo running a subway car and washing skyscraper windows](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/andrew-cuomo-ai-toxic). Mamdani, who went on to win, answered: "Then again, maybe a fake Cuomo is better than the real one?"

The AI fakes that have drawn attention in New York races look like this one: crude, built for ridicule and often meant to be recognized as fakes. New York's 2024 disclosure law was written for the convincing fake that fools voters, and it exempts parody. The fake more likely to cost people their votes is a false message about when, where or how to vote. A label on that message would do little; a fast official correction would do more, and New York City's Board of Elections is already required to run a text and email system that could send one. The Council should give it that job.

## What the law says

The statute, [Election Law 14-106(5)](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._election_law_section_14-106), applies to "materially deceptive media": content that is "indistinguishable from reality to a reasonable person," depicts something that did not happen and was made with AI or other software. Anyone who distributes a political communication containing such media, with "actual knowledge that it is materially deceptive," must label it: "This (image, video, or audio) has been manipulated." Satire and parody are exempt.

There are two ways to enforce it. A candidate whose voice or likeness appears in the media can sue in state Supreme Court for an injunction and legal fees. The case gets an automatic calendar preference and expedited proceedings, and for preliminary relief before an election the court asks whether the candidate is "more likely than not to succeed on the merits," though winning the case requires clear and convincing evidence. The state Board of Elections' chief enforcement counsel can [seek civil penalties](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._election_law_section_14-126) of up to $1,000 or the cost of the communication from anyone who knowingly fails to label a political communication as the law requires. Whether that penalty reaches the AI label, as opposed to the "paid for by" line, has not been tested.

The measure, backed by Gov. Kathy Hochul, passed in the [April 2024 budget](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/ai-election-deepfake-measure-at-risk-in-ny-budget-negotiations). A task force report for the American Political Science Association, edited by Nathaniel Persily and Joshua Tucker, [says the law came partly in response](https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/apsa/event-details/69e2841e810b9dcc8238ea00) to robocalls from then-Mayor Eric Adams, which used an AI tool to have him speak Spanish, Yiddish, Mandarin, Cantonese and Haitian Creole without disclosing that it wasn't him talking.

## The Blakeman complaint

The governor's race is giving the law one of its first tests. Bruce Blakeman, the Republican nominee, ran a [43-second ad](https://www.wsoctv.com/news/politics/ai-ads-are-popular/NEGEBTLILQ4QHLUI5VYFX4YJPM/) in which AI versions of Mamdani and Hochul stroll through a park trading lines that make them look bad. The "paid for by" line runs throughout, according to The Associated Press, and the use of AI is never mentioned. In August, state Democratic Party Chair Jay Jacobs [filed a complaint](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/nys/central-ny/politics/2026/08/11/democrats-blakeman-ai-depictions-) with the state Board of Elections. Blakeman's answer: "everybody knows it's AI and it's funny." Jeffrey Wice of New York Law School told Spectrum News it would be "one of the first times" the board had been asked to weigh in. No ruling had been reported as of mid-October.

Blakeman's argument tracks the statute, which reaches fakes a reasonable person would take for real and exempts parody, so an absurd video is easier to defend than a believable one. If the board reads the law that way, a campaign can show voters a damaging fake of an opponent without a label, as long as the fake is ridiculous.

## What the evidence says about persuasion

The evidence that deepfakes change votes is thin. Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan, writing for the Knight First Amendment Institute, went through [all 78 AI election cases](https://knightcolumbia.org/blog/we-looked-at-78-election-deepfakes-political-misinformation-is-not-an-ai-problem) that WIRED collected in 2024 and found no intent to deceive in half. The political science association's report concludes that deepfakes [played little role](https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/apsa/event-details/69e2841e810b9dcc8238ea00) in the 2024 U.S. campaign and that what spread instead was "AI slop" that is "readily recognized as fake."

The other feared harm is the "liar's dividend," a politician waving off real evidence as fake. In [five survey experiments with more than 15,000 adults](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073), Kaylyn Jackson Schiff, Daniel Schiff and Natália Bueno showed people hypothetical politician responses to real scandals. Crying misinformation raised support when the scandal was reported in text but was "largely ineffective against video evidence." These are survey findings, and in them the excuse worked better against a news story than against video. The association report's authors add that people "still do not instinctively disbelieve" the images and videos they see.

## The case for a tougher law

The volume is rising. The Wesleyan Media Project found less than $50,000 in AI ad spending in the 2024 cycle; by mid-October 2026 it had counted [more than $137 million across 220 ads in 34 states](https://mediaproject.wesleyan.edu/releases-101526/), and 27% of those ads carried a disclaimer. The association report's authors caution that their findings may have low "temporal validity" and that people's ability to spot AI images "is likely to degrade." A convincing fake released the weekend before a close Council primary is easy to imagine.

That worry is behind Hochul's January proposal to [ban non-consensual deepfakes of opposing candidates](https://gothamist.com/news/ny-gov-hochul-wants-to-ban-ai-images-in-political-campaigns) in [set periods before an election](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-unveils-proposals-protect-consumers-and-workers), and to bar knowingly spreading false information that might deter people from voting. Both pieces were [cut from the budget](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S9005/amendment/C) enacted in May, and the proposal did not pass this year.

Labeling rules have not clearly changed behavior where they exist. Travis Ridout, a co-director of the Wesleyan project, cross-referenced its September count with state laws for the AP. In states with AI disclosure laws, [29% of AI ads carried a disclaimer](https://www.wsoctv.com/news/politics/ai-ads-are-popular/NEGEBTLILQ4QHLUI5VYFX4YJPM/). In states without them, 32% did. He noted caveats: some ads fell outside narrow laws, and some ran before a law took effect.

New York's enforcement is faster than ordinary litigation, but every route starts after the media is out, and an injunction stops distribution only from that point on. Cuomo's video was down within five minutes, and one copy still drew more than 2 million views. The candidate's lawsuit also belongs only to a candidate whose voice or likeness appears, so it does not reach a false message about voting that depicts no candidate.

## Lies about how to vote

The cases with the clearest path to lost votes were about the mechanics of voting. In January 2024, robocalls with an AI-cloned Biden voice reached [thousands of New Hampshire homes](https://www.nhpr.org/nh-news/2025-06-13/political-operative-fake-biden-robocalls-nh-primary-found-not-guilty) and told Democrats to "save their vote for the November election." The Federal Communications Commission [fined the consultant behind the calls, Steve Kramer, $6 million](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-issues-6m-fine-nh-robocalls/rosenworcel-statement) in September 2024, and a jury acquitted him on all 22 criminal counts in June 2025. New York has had its own version without any AI: in 2020, according to Hochul's office, [thousands of New Yorkers got automated calls](https://gothamist.com/news/ny-gov-hochul-wants-to-ban-ai-images-in-political-campaigns) falsely warning that their mail-in votes would be sent to the police.

What those voters needed was a fast, official correction.

New York City has a channel for one, on paper. Section 1057-d of the [City Charter](https://www.nyccfb.info/law/charter/) requires the city's Board of Elections to text and email registered voters who sign up, for a fixed list of purposes: election dates and hours, poll sites "and any changes thereto," in-person absentee voting, mail-ballot application deadlines and sample ballots. Nothing in that list covers a warning that a message going around is false.

The Council could add one: when a false claim about voting dates, places, eligibility or ballot handling is circulating, the board sends a correction to affected voters within a set number of hours. Most of those corrections would concern facts the board already has to send, such as dates, hours and poll sites. It would still ask the board to act within hours and get it right, and the Council would first need to learn how many voters have signed up for the alerts. The state Board of Elections should also rule on the Blakeman complaint before Election Day, so campaigns in future races know how it reads the satire exemption.
