At the start of October 2025, Andrew Cuomo's mayoral campaign released [an ad, made with artificial intelligence, in which a synthetic Cuomo drives a subway train and washes high-rise windows](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/mamdani-roasts-cuomo-ai-ad), admitting he might not be cut out for those jobs but insisting he was ready to be mayor. Zohran Mamdani answered that Cuomo had made the ad "the same way he wrote his housing policy: with AI," and added: "Then again, maybe a fake Cuomo is better than the real one?"

Three weeks later, during the Oct. 22 debate, the campaign posted another AI video. It opened on an [AI-generated Mamdani eating rice with his bare hands](https://www.amny.com/politics/cuomo-backlash-ai-generated-ad/), then rolled through a cast of synthetic criminals — a shoplifter in a keffiyeh, a pimp, a drunk driver — all happy to endorse him. A spokesman said a junior staffer had posted an unapproved draft and that it came down five minutes later. By then the journalist Prem Thakker had re-uploaded it on X, where his copy drew more than 2 million views. Common Cause New York said the video may have broken state law by not disclosing its use of AI, though [404 Media later reported](https://www.404media.co/bruce-blakeman-ai-campaign-video-mamdani-hochul/) that it was labeled. No lawsuit over it surfaced, and Mamdani went on to win.

The AI fakes that have drawn attention in New York races look like these: crude, built for ridicule and meant to be recognized. New York's 2024 disclosure law was written for the opposite case, a convincing fake that fools voters, and it exempts parody. The fake more likely to cost people their votes is a false message about when, where or how to vote, and a label would do little against it. What helps is a fast official correction. New York City's Board of Elections is already required to text and email voters who sign up, and the Council should make it send one.

## What the law covers

The statute, [Election Law 14-106(5)](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._election_law_section_14-106), reaches media that a reasonable person could not tell from reality, that shows something that did not happen and that was made with AI or other software. Anyone who knowingly distributes such media in a political communication must label it as manipulated. Satire and parody are exempt. A candidate who appears in the fake can sue in state Supreme Court for an injunction and gets a fast track there. Separately, the state Board of Elections' enforcement counsel can [seek civil penalties](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._election_law_section_14-126) when a political communication lacks a label the law requires, though whether that reaches the AI label has never been tested.

The measure, backed by Gov. Kathy Hochul, passed in the [April 2024 budget](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/ai-election-deepfake-measure-at-risk-in-ny-budget-negotiations). A draft report from the American Political Science Association's presidential task force on AI, edited by its co-chairs, Nathaniel Persily and Joshua Tucker, [says the law came partly in response](https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/apsa/event-details/69e2841e810b9dcc8238ea00) to robocalls from then-Mayor Eric Adams that used an AI tool to have him speak Spanish, Yiddish, Mandarin and other languages he does not speak, without telling listeners it wasn't him.

The governor's race is giving the law one of its first tests. Bruce Blakeman, the Republican nominee, ran [an ad](https://www.wsoctv.com/news/politics/ai-ads-are-popular/NEGEBTLILQ4QHLUI5VYFX4YJPM/) in which AI versions of Mamdani and Hochul stroll through a park trading lines that make them look bad; The Associated Press found that it never mentions AI. In August, state Democratic Party Chair Jay Jacobs [filed a complaint](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/nys/central-ny/politics/2026/08/11/democrats-blakeman-ai-depictions-) with the state Board of Elections. Blakeman's answer: "everybody knows it's AI and it's funny." Jeffrey Wice of New York Law School told Spectrum News it would be one of the first times the board had been asked to weigh in. No ruling had been reported by mid-October.

Blakeman's defense tracks the statute. A law that covers only fakes a reasonable person would believe, and exempts parody, protects the absurd fake better than the believable one. If the board reads it that way, a campaign can show voters a damaging fake of an opponent with no label, so long as the fake is ridiculous. The board should rule before Election Day so campaigns in later races know where the line sits.

## Mockery, mostly

The evidence that deepfakes change votes is thin. Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan, writing for the Knight First Amendment Institute, went through [the 78 AI election cases](https://knightcolumbia.org/blog/we-looked-at-78-election-deepfakes-political-misinformation-is-not-an-ai-problem) that WIRED collected in 2024 and found no intent to deceive in half of them. The task force report concludes that deepfakes [played little role](https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/apsa/event-details/69e2841e810b9dcc8238ea00) in the 2024 U.S. campaign and that what spread instead was "AI slop" that is "readily recognized as fake." The "liar's dividend," a politician waving off real evidence as fake, shows up in experiments, but mostly against the written word. In [survey experiments](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073) by Kaylyn Jackson Schiff, Daniel Schiff and Natália Bueno, crying misinformation raised a politician's support when a scandal was reported in text and was "largely ineffective against video evidence."

None of this will hold still. The Wesleyan Media Project found less than $50,000 spent on AI ads in the 2024 cycle; by mid-October 2026 it had counted [more than $137 million](https://mediaproject.wesleyan.edu/releases-101526/) and found that most of those ads carried no disclaimer. The task force authors warn that people's ability to spot AI images "is likely to degrade." That prospect is behind Hochul's January proposal to [ban non-consensual deepfakes of opposing candidates](https://gothamist.com/news/ny-gov-hochul-wants-to-ban-ai-images-in-political-campaigns) in [set periods before an election](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-unveils-proposals-protect-consumers-and-workers), along with a ban on knowingly spreading false information that might deter people from voting. Both were [cut from the budget](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S9005/amendment/C) enacted in May.

Labels, meanwhile, have not clearly changed behavior where states require them. Travis Ridout, a co-director of the Wesleyan project, matched its September count against state laws for the AP and found that [29% of AI ads in states with disclosure laws carried a disclaimer](https://www.wsoctv.com/news/politics/ai-ads-are-popular/NEGEBTLILQ4QHLUI5VYFX4YJPM/), against 32% in states without them. Some ads fell outside narrow laws, he noted, and some ran before a law took effect.

If Albany takes up Hochul's package again, the voter-deception ban is the half the evidence supports. Every enforcement route in the current law starts after the fake is out, and an injunction stops distribution only from then on; Cuomo's video was down in five minutes, and copies kept circulating. The candidate's lawsuit also belongs only to a candidate who appears in the fake, so it does nothing about a false message on voting that depicts no candidate at all.

## Lies about how to vote

In January 2024, robocalls with an AI-cloned Biden voice reached [thousands of New Hampshire homes](https://www.nhpr.org/nh-news/2025-06-13/political-operative-fake-biden-robocalls-nh-primary-found-not-guilty) just before the primary. "It's important that you save your vote for the November election," the [fake Biden told them](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-104A1.pdf). The Federal Communications Commission [fined the consultant behind the calls, Steve Kramer, $6 million](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-issues-6m-fine-nh-robocalls/rosenworcel-statement); a jury later acquitted him of all criminal charges. New York has had its own version without any AI: in 2020, according to Hochul's office, [thousands of New Yorkers got automated calls](https://gothamist.com/news/ny-gov-hochul-wants-to-ban-ai-images-in-political-campaigns) falsely warning that their mail-in votes would be sent to the police.

What those voters needed was a fast, official correction. New York City has a channel for one, on paper. Section 1057-d of the [City Charter](https://www.nyccfb.info/law/charter/) requires the Board of Elections to text and email registered voters who sign up, with sample ballots, mail-ballot deadlines and each election's dates, hours and poll sites, including any changes. Nothing on that list covers a warning that a message going around is false.

The Council should add one: when a false claim about voting dates, places, eligibility or ballot handling is circulating, the board sends a correction to affected voters within a set number of hours. Most of those corrections would restate facts the board already has to send. The hard part is speed, since the board would have to confirm what is circulating and answer it the same day. Before writing the deadline into law, the Council should find out how many voters have signed up for the alerts, because a correction reaches only the people on the list.
