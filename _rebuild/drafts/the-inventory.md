On Oct. 5, the New York City Council sat as a Committee of the Whole to question the companies that build artificial intelligence (AI). Senior people from Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI [agreed to testify](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/10/13/3295/), and the hearing [ran from 11:09 a.m. to past 9 p.m.](https://nyc.legistar.com/View.ashx?M=F&ID=16072121&GUID=B2DB4127-3040-4B2C-9BDC-5DE47D30860D) The Council counted 46 members taking part. (Anthropic makes the model that wrote this essay.)

The City's own algorithms got far less of the day. Sarah Milstein, a deputy commissioner at the Office of Technology and Innovation (OTI), [told members](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/media/Advocacy-Testimony-Intros-161-T20262602-T20262601-T20262606-T20262605-T20262599-T20262603-504-T20262600.pdf) that the new Office of Algorithmic Accountability had been formally set up, with six jobs funded in the current budget and hiring underway. Speaker Julie Menin answered that the office was supposed to be running by June. She also raised the departure of Jiahao Chen, who had been OTI's director of AI and machine learning for two and a half years and had resigned a week or so earlier; Menin said press reports tied his exit to frustration over positions held up by the City's budget office. Milstein said OTI hoped to name a director for the new office "in the next week or two," [amNewYork reported](https://www.amny.com/politics/nyc-ai-cyberattacks-safeguards/). None had been announced by Oct. 20.

When the office does open, it will inherit a catalog. Under [Local Law 35 of 2022](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=4265421&GUID=FBA29B34-9266-4B52-B438-A772D81B1CB5), every agency reports each year the algorithmic tools it uses to make decisions that affect the public. The [open dataset](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/City-Government/Algorithmic-Tools-Compliance-Report/jaw4-yuem) has nearly tripled since the first report, to 86 tools at 19 agencies for 2025. Each entry says what the tool does, what data it uses, whether a vendor was involved, when the agency started using it and, starting with the 2025 report, how much it acts on its own. The state does far less. Albany's [inventory](https://data.ny.gov/Government-Finance/NYS-AI-Systems-Inventory-Beginning-September-2025/fgvt-qr4i) runs five columns, and [New York Focus found](https://nysfocus.com/2026/07/30/new-york-ai-systems-inventory-findings) that it leaves out the Department of Motor Vehicles' facial recognition and the State Police entirely.

What the City's list almost never says is whether a tool works. Take the Administration for Children's Services (ACS), which uses a model to help pick which child-protection investigations get one of its scarce quality-assurance reviews, about 3,000 out of roughly 50,000 cases a year. The entry explains that the model was trained on years of closed investigations and tested on a held-out fifth of them. It never says how the test came out.

That silence holds across every tool that sorts people or cases. Agencies themselves classify 26 of their 2025 tools as allocating resources, triaging, managing risk or evaluating performance. Several entries mention testing, back-testing or validation; none gives an accuracy rate, an error rate or any other result. In a review of all 86 entries, Vital City found one measured outcome, and it belongs to a help-desk chatbot at the public schools that resets passwords and opens tickets. It handles about 30% of voice calls on its own.

The performance numbers that exist tend to come from auditors. The entry for ShotSpotter, the New York Police Department's (NYPD) gunshot detection system, says its training data belongs to the vendor, a line repeated across dozens of entries, and the NYPD files the system under data management rather than among the decision tools. A [2024 City comptroller audit](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system) found that ShotSpotter alerts led to confirmed shootings between 8% and 20% of the time, on a product the department had spent more than $45 million on. A [2023 state comptroller audit](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2023-21n10.pdf) found the NYPD had never reviewed federal accuracy testing of its facial recognition software or decided what level of accuracy it would accept. This year the state comptroller came back and found the City still lacks a complete inventory of the AI its agencies use, [City & State reported](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/08/state-audit-finds-room-improvement-nycs-ai-governance/415734/).

The City has promised to fix this before. In 2019, the [Automated Decision Systems Task Force](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/adstaskforce/downloads/pdf/ADS-Report-11192019.pdf) urged the City's oversight body to publish the outcomes of its assessments. Its members argued over whether they could examine real City systems and in the end reviewed four. City & State later called it ["a slow-moving body."](https://www.cityandstateny.com/politics/2025/11/new-york-city-will-try-again-regulate-ai/409803/) OTI's own [AI Action Plan](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/reports/artificial-intelligence-action-plan.pdf) in 2023 floated adding performance to the yearly reports and promised reviews of whether tools in use meet their goals. Its [2025 progress report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/AI-Action-Plan-Progress-Report-Year-2.pdf) marks the reporting item complete, though the dataset still has no field for performance, and lists the reviews as still in progress.

The 2025 laws built oversight without measurement. [Local Law 188](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6557537&GUID=14A351D1-8AED-448E-A8C0-355A39CB02BE) created the accountability office and gave it the power to suspend a tool. But its test is whether a tool risks discriminatory decisions and whether it meets compliance standards the office has yet to write, and Menin told OTI at the hearing that it had done "no rulemaking whatsoever." Neither question asks whether the tool does the job the agency uses it for. The law added two items to the yearly report, whether a tool changed and a summary of any assessment by the new office. [Local Law 195](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6860202&GUID=C8F189B6-FFA8-4539-86BF-78436E7BE335) requires the office to post, by March 2027, a list of systems it has reviewed before they go into use, and the civic technology group BetaNYC [asked at the hearing](https://www.beta.nyc/2026/10/05/council-ai-hearing-2026/) when that list would appear.

OTI's answer to all of this is patience. Milstein told the Council that OTI is "fully compliant with the law" and that the office, a few months old, is "progressing as planned." Outcomes are harder to define than descriptions, and a hit rate printed without context can mislead. An agency that picks its own measure can pick a flattering one. Those are reasons to start with the 26 tools that sort people and cases, where agencies already run the tests. ACS held out more than 200,000 cases to check its model. The Department of Correction back-tests its jail population forecast. The Fire Department checks its ambulance hospital-suggestion algorithm against five years of transport records. The reporting law already lets an agency withhold anything that would endanger public safety or compromise an active investigation.

Other governments have not solved this. Amsterdam's register, now folded into the [Dutch national one](https://algoritmes.overheid.nl/nl/organisatie/gemeente-amsterdam), describes the [scan cars that enforce parking](https://algoritmes.overheid.nl/nl/algoritme/gm0363/78451490/fiscaal-handhaven-parkeercontrole), lists the privacy and ethics reviews done on them and names misread license plates as the main risk, with no error rate. The [federal inventory](https://github.com/ombegov/2025-Federal-Agency-AI-Use-Case-Inventory) asks whether "high-impact" uses were tested before launch; for about one in five of those in use, the answer was yes.

The Council has a bill already open. [Int. 161](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/09/25/3252/), from Council Member Carmen De La Rosa, would add to each entry a count of City employees whose jobs the tool affected. It was [laid over](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=7861810&GUID=34B70F41-7D60-4866-9434-7EB17E68661C) on Oct. 5, and as drafted it rewrites the list of required items from the 2022 text, which would erase the two items Local Law 188 added. BetaNYC [flagged that](https://www.beta.nyc/2026/10/05/council-ai-hearing-2026/) in its testimony. The revision should restore both and add a results column: for every tool that allocates, triages, manages risk or evaluates performance, the measure the agency uses to judge it, the latest result and the date. "Not measured" should be a permitted answer, printed in the same column.

A blank in that column should carry consequences. The accountability office can already suspend a tool. Its director, once named, should start with the "not measured" entries and give each agency a deadline to produce a result, and the revised bill should make an unmeasured decision tool grounds for suspension. The office, not the agency, should also decide which tools count as decision tools, so that a system like ShotSpotter cannot sit outside the column because of how its owner filed it. A City Hall spokesperson, Jeremy Edwards, told City & State in July that the Mamdani administration would use AI ["only where it demonstrably improves life for New Yorkers."](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) For the 26 decision tools, the City's own list does not yet show it.
