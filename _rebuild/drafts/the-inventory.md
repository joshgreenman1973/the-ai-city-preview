On Oct. 5, the New York City Council sat as a Committee of the Whole to question the companies that build artificial intelligence (AI). Senior people from Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI [agreed to testify](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/10/13/3295/), and the hearing ran nearly 10 hours, with 46 council members taking part. (Anthropic makes the model that wrote this essay.)

The City's own algorithms got a few paragraphs. Sarah Milstein, a deputy commissioner at the Office of Technology and Innovation (OTI), [told members](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/media/Advocacy-Testimony-Intros-161-T20262602-T20262601-T20262606-T20262605-T20262599-T20262603-504-T20262600.pdf) that the new Office of Algorithmic Accountability "has been formally established," with six positions funded in the fiscal 2027 budget and hiring "actively underway." OTI hoped to name a director "in the next week or two," [amNewYork reported](https://www.amny.com/politics/nyc-ai-cyberattacks-safeguards/). As of Oct. 19, none had been named. Speaker Julie Menin said the office was supposed to be operating by June.

The new office will oversee tools the City already catalogs. Under [Local Law 35 of 2022](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=4265421&GUID=FBA29B34-9266-4B52-B438-A772D81B1CB5), every agency reports each year the algorithmic tools it uses to make decisions that affect the public. The [open dataset](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/City-Government/Algorithmic-Tools-Compliance-Report/jaw4-yuem) has grown from 31 tools in 2022 to 86 in 2025, reported by 19 agencies. Each entry describes the tool, the data behind it, whether a vendor was involved and when the agency started using it. Starting with the 2025 report, each also rates how much the tool acts on its own: 40 are "informative," 23 supervised, 11 monitored and 12 fully autonomous, a category made up mostly of captioning, transcription and translation software.

The state offers much less. Albany's [inventory](https://data.ny.gov/Government-Finance/NYS-AI-Systems-Inventory-Beginning-September-2025/fgvt-qr4i) lists 19 systems at 13 agencies in five columns, and [New York Focus found](https://nysfocus.com/2026/07/30/new-york-ai-systems-inventory-findings) that it leaves out the Department of Motor Vehicles' facial recognition. The State Police do not appear at all.

But the City's list says a great deal about what each tool is and almost nothing about whether it works. The next change to the law should require agencies to report results for the tools that rank and score New Yorkers, and to say so in writing when they have never checked.

## The 26 decision tools

The Administration for Children's Services (ACS) runs a model that helps choose which of about 50,000 child-protection investigations a year get one of roughly 3,000 extra quality-assurance reviews. The entry says the model was trained on 815,284 records from closed investigations and tested on 205,241 more. It does not say how the test came out.

The same is true of all 26 tools that agencies themselves classify as allocating resources, triaging cases, managing risk or evaluating performance. Several entries say a model was tested, back-tested or validated. None reports an accuracy rate, an error rate or any other measured result. Across all 86 entries for 2025, Vital City found one measured outcome. It belongs to the public schools' technology help desk chatbot, which resets passwords and opens tickets and, the entry says, fully handles about 30% of voice calls without a human.

Where performance numbers exist, they tend to come from auditors. The entry for ShotSpotter, the New York Police Department's (NYPD) gunshot detection system, says its training data "is proprietary to the vendor," a phrase that, in one form or another, appears in 38 of the 86 entries. It took a [2024 City comptroller audit](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/audit-report-on-the-new-york-city-police-departments-oversight-of-its-agreement-with-shotspotter-inc-for-the-gunshot-detection-and-location-system) to establish that ShotSpotter alerts resulted in confirmed shootings between 8% and 20% of the time, for a product the department had spent more than $45 million on. In 2023, a [state comptroller audit](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/state-agencies/audits/pdf/sga-2023-21n10.pdf) found that the NYPD had not reviewed federal accuracy testing of its facial recognition software, "nor did it establish what level of accuracy would be acceptable." A follow-up from the state comptroller this year found that the City "still does not maintain a complete inventory of AI tools used across agencies," [City & State reported](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/08/state-audit-finds-room-improvement-nycs-ai-governance/415734/) in August.

## Earlier recommendations

In November 2019, the [Automated Decision Systems (ADS) Task Force](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/adstaskforce/downloads/pdf/ADS-Report-11192019.pdf), created under Local Law 49 of 2018, recommended that the City's oversight body publish "key performance indicators" for its work on automated systems and the "outcomes of any ADS assessments." The task force had trouble seeing the systems it was meant to study. Members argued over whether they could review real examples, and in the end they looked at four. City & State later called it ["a slow-moving body."](https://www.cityandstateny.com/politics/2025/11/new-york-city-will-try-again-regulate-ai/409803/)

OTI's [AI Action Plan](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/reports/artificial-intelligence-action-plan.pdf), released in October 2023, made the point twice. Action 1.5 proposed expanding the Local Law 35 reports, possibly by "adding reporting requirements related to models and performance." Action 1.8 promised reviews of tools in operation that "will assess the tool's effectiveness at fulfilling its stated goals." The plan's [October 2025 progress report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/AI-Action-Plan-Progress-Report-Year-2.pdf) marks 1.5 complete, though no performance field appeared in the dataset. Action 1.8 is listed as in progress, one of eight such items among the plan's 37 actions.

The 2025 laws add oversight and stop short of measurement. [Local Law 188](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6557537&GUID=14A351D1-8AED-448E-A8C0-355A39CB02BE), which created the accountability office, gives it power to suspend a tool. Its central test is whether a tool risks "discriminatory decision-making" and whether it meets standards the office has yet to write. Neither question asks whether the tool does the job the agency uses it for. The law also added two items to the annual report: whether a tool was modified and a summary of any assessment by the new office. [Local Law 195](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6860202&GUID=C8F189B6-FFA8-4539-86BF-78436E7BE335) requires the office to post, by March 31, 2027, a list of systems it has assessed before deployment. At the Oct. 5 hearing, the civic technology group BetaNYC [asked](https://www.beta.nyc/2026/10/05/council-ai-hearing-2026/) when that list would appear.

## The case for waiting

Outcomes are harder to define than descriptions, and for captioning software, "did it work" is a question about typos. A published hit rate without context can mislead, and an agency that picks its own metric can pick a flattering one. OTI has also argued that new reporting can duplicate work already underway: It told the Council that a separate bill adding job-impact figures to the report "would not produce new insights" beyond a study the office is conducting. Milstein said OTI is "fully compliant with the law" and that the accountability office, now a few months old, is "progressing as planned."

Nobody else has solved this either. Amsterdam's algorithm register now lives on the [Dutch national register](https://algoritmes.overheid.nl/nl/organisatie/gemeente-amsterdam). Its [entry for the city's parking-enforcement scan cars](https://algoritmes.overheid.nl/nl/algoritme/gm0363/78451490/fiscaal-handhaven-parkeercontrole) names the privacy and ethics reviews performed and says the main risk is misreading a license plate. It gives no error rate. The [federal inventory](https://github.com/ombegov/2025-Federal-Agency-AI-Use-Case-Inventory) asks whether pre-deployment testing was done for "high-impact" uses, with "yes" and "in progress" among the answers. Of 227 such uses marked deployed, 44 answered yes.

Those objections argue for a narrow requirement, limited to the 26 decision tools, where there is a result to measure. Agencies already run the tests. ACS held out 205,241 cases to check its model, the Department of Correction back-tests its jail population forecast, and the Fire Department compares its ambulance hospital-suggestion algorithm with five years of transport records. And the reporting law already lets an agency withhold anything that would endanger public safety or interfere with an active investigation.

## A results column

The Council has a bill that opens the report. [Int. 161](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/09/25/3252/), from Council Member Carmen De La Rosa, would add a count of City employees whose jobs were affected by each tool. It was [laid over in committee](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=7861810&GUID=34B70F41-7D60-4866-9434-7EB17E68661C) on Oct. 5. As drafted, it rewrites the list of required items starting from the 2022 text, which would drop the two items Local Law 188 added, including the summary of the accountability office's assessment. BetaNYC [pointed this out](https://www.beta.nyc/2026/10/05/council-ai-hearing-2026/) in its testimony. A revised bill should keep both items and add one more.

For any tool an agency classifies as resource allocation, triage, risk management or performance evaluation, the report should state the measure the agency uses to judge whether the tool works, the latest result and the date it was taken. "Not measured" should be an allowed answer, printed in the same column. A City Hall spokesperson, Jeremy Edwards, told City & State in July that the Mamdani administration would use AI ["only where it demonstrably improves life for New Yorkers."](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) The accountability office could start its reviews with the tools whose entry says "not measured."
