On Friday, July 17, 2015, a program at the New York City Health Department flagged eight cases of Legionnaires' disease bunched together in the South Bronx "[before any human public health monitor noticed it](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/22/10/16-0097_article)." The program runs every day on free software called SaTScan, which looks for reported cases of disease that cluster in place and time. Investigators traced the outbreak to a cooling tower on top of a new hotel. It [sickened 138 people and killed 16](https://www.healthbeat.org/newyork/2025/08/20/legionnaires-disease-outbreak-lessons-learned-inspections).

The software did its job, which was to notice people who were already sick. Prevention fell to a new law. Since August 2015, owners have had to [register their cooling towers](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/industry/cooling-tower-information-registration.page) before running them and test them for Legionella bacteria. The Health Department began inspecting towers in 2017 and that year reached [5,200 of them](https://gothamist.com/news/legionnaires-inspections-sank-to-near-record-low-ahead-of-nyc-outbreak), more than the 4,928 that were registered in 2025.

Other agencies took a different route to deciding where inspectors go. In November 2019, the Fire Department began scoring buildings with a model called ALARM (A Learning Approach to Risk Modeling) to [schedule its fire inspections](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?year=2025&agency=Fire%20Department&computation_type=Scoring), using dispatch records, building data, 311 calls and nearby felony crimes. The approach dates to the Bloomberg administration, when City analysts ranked complaints about illegally converted apartments. Michael Flowers, who ran that work, wrote in 2012 that the rate of vacate orders on those inspections "[jumped from 13 percent to 70 percent](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/blog/2012/09/27/efficiency-through-strategic-data-analysis)." Research for this essay turned up no independent test of his figure or of ALARM.

Chicago has had one. Since 2015 its health department had ranked restaurants and other food businesses by their odds of a critical violation, and in a pilot the model found violators [seven and a half days earlier](https://chicago.github.io/food-inspections-evaluation/) on average. In the fall of 2019, researchers at the Illinois Institute of Technology [took it apart](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04906). The model's most influential input was which group of inspectors had last visited. A restaurant last seen by the group that cited critical violations most often went near the front of the line, even if that visit had found nothing serious. Those inspectors may have been stricter, or their routes riskier; either way, the authors wrote, a restaurant has no control over who inspects it. They could not even say whether the model had sped anything up, since hit rates rose after 2015 on complaint inspections as well as routine ones.

Under Local Law 35 of 2022, City agencies must list their algorithmic tools each year, and the Health Department has [listed more than any other agency](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/City-Government/Algorithmic-Tools-Compliance-Report/jaw4-yuem). Of its 17 entries for 2025, 13 are Public Health Laboratory programs that assemble and compare genomes, including the software it uses to sequence Legionella. None ranks buildings, restaurants or cooling towers for inspection. SaTScan appears in no year's list. The one tool aimed at restaurants, which scores Yelp reviews for signs of food poisoning, has been [unable to receive data](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?year=2025&tool_name=Improving%20Foodborne%20Disease%20Outbreak%20Detection%20by%20Incorporating%20Complaints%20Identified%20in%20Social%20Media%20Data) since January 2025.

That year the inspections fell away. The department had done [only 1,167](https://gothamist.com/news/legionnaires-inspections-sank-to-near-record-low-ahead-of-nyc-outbreak) by June, and a spokesperson blamed a shortage of inspectors. By August it had [13 fewer than in 2022](https://gothamist.com/news/nyc-lost-health-inspectors-in-years-preceding-harlem-legionnaires-disease-outbreak), even though the Independent Budget Office found that the pool of money that funds them had grown by nearly a third.

On July 25, 2025, the department's software flagged a cluster of positive tests among people living in Central Harlem. The outbreak reached [118 confirmed cases and seven deaths](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/legionella/public-health-alerts-outbreak-legionnaires-disease-associated-cooling-tower-systems). Seven of the 10 buildings at its center had gone a year without a City inspection. One of the towers whose bacteria matched the patients' was unregistered and had skipped required testing.

The department's spokesperson said that it would be wrong to claim the outbreak "[could have been prevented through more staff and inspections](https://gothamist.com/news/nyc-lost-health-inspectors-in-years-preceding-harlem-legionnaires-disease-outbreak)." The City had not visited most of the buildings that turned out to matter, though, and no inspector could visit a tower the City did not know existed.

In October 2025, the Public Engagement Unit began using a model built by the Department of Housing Preservation and Development to [pick buildings for door knocking](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?year=2025&tool_name=Tenant%20Harassment%20Risk%20Model%20%28THRM%29) on tenant harassment. It scores buildings partly on 311 complaints, as ALARM does. Complaint data carry a known bias. In Kansas City, Missouri, Constantine Kontokosta and Boyeong Hong found that low-income and minority neighborhoods were [less likely to report street and nuisance problems](https://marroninstitute.nyu.edu/papers/bias-in-smart-city-governance-how-socio-spatial-disparities-in-311-complaint-behavior-impact-the-fairness-of-data-driven-decisions) even though their need was greater. A model that leans on 311 can send inspectors to the neighborhoods that call most.

After Harlem, the City hired. The tower inspection team grew to [54 by May 2026](https://www.habitatmag.com/Publication-Content/Building-Operations/2026/May-2026/cooling-tower-inspectors-legionnaires), though Gothamist reported that the new hires faced months of training and [did no inspections in May or June](https://gothamist.com/news/nyc-legionella-inspections-rose-only-gradually-before-latest-legionnaires-cluster-in-manhattan). In mid-April, more than half the city's towers had gone a year without a visit. A new rule that took effect in May requires owners to test running towers monthly instead of quarterly.

That summer a cluster on the Upper East Side reached [94 cases and 11 deaths](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-announces-sweeping-new-measures-to-strengthen-new-.html) by the end of August. The tower with the most violations, at 300 E. 83rd St., was unregistered, and its owners had done no Legionella sampling for more than a year. Investigators found it, the department said, "[by comparing Department of Buildings construction permit records with cooling tower registration data](https://www.nyc.gov/site/doh/about/press/pr2026/health-department-declares-ues-legionnaires-exposure-has-ended.page)."

On Sept. 1, Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced that the Health Department and the Department of Buildings would [strengthen the connection between the tower registry and the buildings department's construction permitting system](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-announces-sweeping-new-measures-to-strengthen-new-.html) by the end of the year. The release described the change as formalizing a partnership that had already turned up unregistered towers, and it promised a public map of registered towers and their inspection records by spring 2027.

Nine days later, the department opened an investigation into another cluster, in Melrose and Morrisania in the South Bronx, where the 2015 outbreak struck. By Oct. 13 it had reached [24 cases and two deaths](https://www.nyc.gov/site/doh/health/health-topics/legionnaires-disease.page). Seven tower systems in the area held live bacteria, and all were [cleaned within days](https://www.nyc.gov/site/doh/about/press/pr2026/south-bronx-legionnaires-cluster-source-eliminated.page). Genetic matching to find the source was still under way.

The permit match is the right idea, and the City has now committed to it. The announcement frames it as a way to get towers registered "before they are ever turned on." The tower on East 83rd Street was already in place. The match should also run back through past permit records to find every tower operating off the books today, and the department should publish how many it finds and how soon each one gets an inspector. Both sets of records already belong to the City, and neither depends on anyone calling 311.

The larger choice is whether to rank cooling towers at all. Ranking is how an agency rations visits it cannot make. The Fire Department, with about 330,000 buildings to cover, has to choose. The tower list is short: Gothamist counts [nearly 6,000](https://gothamist.com/news/nyc-legionella-inspections-rose-only-gradually-before-latest-legionnaires-cluster-in-manhattan) towers in the city, and the department inspected 5,200 in 2017. It says it [aims to inspect every registered tower each year](https://www.nyc.gov/site/doh/about/press/pr2026/second-death-in-ues-legionnaires-disease-cluster.page). The City should pay for enough trained inspectors to do that and report every year how close it came. A ranking model for towers would mostly decide which ones to skip.

The agencies that do have to rank should prove their models work. ALARM, the harassment model and any tool like them should send one visit in 10 to buildings chosen at random and report the violation rate on those visits next to the rate on ranked visits in their annual Local Law 35 filings. Chicago needed outside researchers and published code to learn what its model was leaning on. If the random visits find violations about as often as the ranked ones, the model is adding little, and the agency should stop using it.
