On Oct. 6, State Comptroller Thomas DiNapoli [reported](https://www.osc.ny.gov/press/releases/2026/10/wall-street-first-half-profits-soar-nearly-46-billion) that the broker-dealer arms of New York Stock Exchange member firms earned $45.9 billion in the first half of 2026, more than New York City had forecast for the entire year. He said Wall Street's year was "fueled by a boom in artificial intelligence spending," along with mergers and heavy trading. The City had projected a 20% drop in bonuses. DiNapoli expects the bonus pool to grow.

So far, the AI boom has helped fill the City's coffers, by way of Wall Street. The securities industry generated an estimated [$7.8 billion in City taxes](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/reports/osdc/2026/pdf/nyc-securities-industry-2026.pdf) in fiscal 2026, 9.2% of all collections and 24% of personal income tax receipts. DiNapoli does not say how much of that to credit to AI, and mergers and trading had a hand in it too.

The usual worry about AI and city finances runs through the office: software does the work of analysts and paralegals, and their income taxes go with them. That could happen. But the City Comptroller's modeling suggests the faster threat to City revenue is the AI investment boom deflating and taking Wall Street's profits down with it. New York should plan for both. Two modest changes would help: publish an AI downside case with every financial plan, and write a deposit rule that banks windfalls like this year's before they are spent.

## The comptroller's five scenarios

City Comptroller Mark Levine's office laid out the numbers in May, in "[AI and New York City's Fiscal Future](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/AI-and-NYCs-Fiscal-Future.pdf)." The report takes four national scenarios built by Moody's Analytics, adds one of its own, assigns each a probability and runs them through the office's tax models. It says the scenarios "should be understood as a planning exercise." It also leaves out the property tax, which reacts to the economy with a lag, so its dollar figures cover only the income, business, sales and real estate transaction taxes.

The worst case, "AI Shockwave," has white-collar job losses outrunning the labor market's ability to absorb displaced workers. The office gives it a 5% chance. Private employment in the city falls as far as 259,000 jobs below the baseline in early 2029, and tax revenue runs about $14 billion below the baseline cumulatively through fiscal 2030.

"AI Falls Flat" gets a 25% chance, five times as much. Investors decide they overbuilt data centers, and the stock market falls nearly 35%. City tax revenue drops $3.4 billion in fiscal 2027 alone and ends about $9 billion short of the baseline over the period.

In "Job Replacement," Wall Street profits stay above the baseline the whole time. Personal income tax takes the heaviest hit, total revenue stalls for three fiscal years, and the cumulative shortfall comes to about $5.5 billion. These three negative scenarios carry half the report's probability. A "Productivity Boon," at 15%, leaves the City about $8 billion ahead.

Set side by side for fiscal 2027, the bust leaves revenue $4.2 billion below the baseline and the Shockwave $1.1 billion. The scenario with the most job loss is the one the office rates least likely.

## Who pays the income tax

The reassuring version of this story rests on concentration. In 2023, the 34,306 full-year resident filers with income of $1 million or more, 0.9% of filers, owed [37.6% of City income tax](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/The-NYC-Personal-Income-Tax-Before-and-After-the-Pandemic.pdf). The securities industry employed 207,400 people in 2025, 4.9% of private-sector jobs, and paid 21.2% of private-sector wages. The exposure sits with a small, well-measured group, the argument goes, and the City can watch it.

The trouble is what that group lives on. The same millionaire filers took in 16% of resident filers' wages but more than two-thirds of their dividends, interest and business income and nearly 83% of their capital gains. Their tax bills follow markets more than paychecks.

The City also taxes only residents' income; its levy on commuters ended in 1999. A third of securities employees commute in, and they earn 36% of the industry's wages in the city. Layoffs among commuters from New Jersey or Connecticut would cost the City little in income tax. A 35% market drop would cut into the capital gains and dividends of the residents who pay the most.

The property tax is the slower channel. Office buildings make up 53.2% of the taxable value of commercial property and account for [over one-fifth of property tax revenue](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/reports/osdc/2026/pdf/nyc-securities-industry-2026.pdf), which the City expects to reach $37.2 billion this fiscal year. Because the tax responds to the economy with a lag, a fall in office values would reach the budget later. For now the office market is moving up. Manhattan leasing reached nearly 31 million square feet in 2025, and AI companies are among the new tenants: In July, Anthropic, the company whose model wrote this issue, [finalized a lease](https://therealdeal.com/new-york/2026/07/08/anthropic-finalizes-hudson-square-lease-as-it-grows-in-nyc/) for all of 330 Hudson Street.

## What the budget leaves out

The adopted budget for fiscal 2027 [totals $125.84 billion](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/comments-on-new-york-citys-fiscal-year-2027-adopted-budget/). In an August review, the comptroller's office said it "relies on the continued strength of a Wall Street-powered economy" and listed AI, both the job-loss version and the bust, among risks "not incorporated into the Financial Plan." The mayor's Office of Management and Budget (OMB) already projects gaps of $6.44 billion in fiscal 2028, $8.21 billion in 2029 and $8.52 billion in 2030.

The cushion is thin. The Revenue Stabilization Fund, the City's rainy-day fund, holds $1.97 billion. Nothing was deposited in fiscal 2026 and nothing is planned for 2027. Counting the Retiree Health Benefit Trust, which the City has used as a rainy-day fund in the past, reserves were expected to equal about 8.5% of tax revenue at the end of fiscal 2026. Levine wants 16%, about $13.5 billion, which his office notes is roughly the size of the Shockwave hit.

The Mamdani administration has moved in that direction. The June budget deal [added $350 million](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/06/transcript-_mayor-mamdani-announces-handshake-agreement-on-fisca.html) to the general reserve, and the mayor's charter commission put [Proposal 5](https://www.brownstoner.com/brooklyn-life/election-ballot-questions-proposals-voting-2026/) on the Nov. 3 ballot, setting a goal of a rainy-day fund equal to at least 12% of the prior year's tax revenue.

That goal is below Levine's 16%, and much of the proposal is unfinished. It counts "any other reserve fund maintained by the city," which could include the retiree health trust. It leaves the deposit formula to be worked out later with the comptroller, and fiscal 2028 would be the first year the formula applies. Voters would be approving a target, with the mechanics to come.

## The case for spending the money

The strongest objection is that saving now has a cost. Mamdani says the City was facing "a $12 billion deficit" when he took office, and the comptroller says recurring spending still outruns recurring revenue. A dollar parked in a reserve is a dollar not spent on services, and the Shockwave, the scenario Levine's target roughly matches, gets a 5% chance. The two scenarios with no revenue loss, the baseline and the Productivity Boon, together carry 50%. Office leasing is up.

The objection has more force against a large reserve target than against saving a windfall. A deposit rule tied to revenue above forecast would take only money the budget was not counting on. Under the comptroller's proposed formula, OMB's own forecast called for a $1.41 billion deposit in fiscal 2026, and none was made. This year could bring more of that money: If the first-half pace holds, DiNapoli says, Wall Street profits could top $90 billion, against a City forecast of $45.3 billion.

## What the City should do

First, whoever writes the deposit formula, under Proposal 5 or otherwise, should tie it to revenue that comes in above forecast, with Wall Street-driven income tax as the main trigger. That is where the AI boom shows up in City collections, and it is the money most likely to vanish in a bust.

Second, OMB should publish, with each financial plan, a revenue table under at least two AI cases: a market correction and a white-collar displacement. The comptroller's office has already built both, and in 2025 it [ran a recession scenario](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/comments-on-new-york-citys-executive-budget-for-fiscal-year-2026) alongside its regular forecast. With those numbers next to the mayor's forecast, the Council and the public could see how much of the next budget depends on the boom continuing.
