On Oct. 6, State Comptroller Thomas DiNapoli [reported](https://www.osc.ny.gov/press/releases/2026/10/wall-street-first-half-profits-soar-nearly-46-billion) that the broker-dealer arms of New York Stock Exchange member firms earned $45.9 billion in the first half of 2026, more than New York City had forecast for the whole year. He said Wall Street's year was "fueled by a boom in artificial intelligence spending," along with mergers and heavy trading. The City had budgeted for bonuses to shrink. DiNapoli expects them to grow.

That boom has been helping to pay the City's bills. The securities industry generated an estimated [$7.8 billion in City taxes](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/reports/osdc/2026/pdf/nyc-securities-industry-2026.pdf) in fiscal 2026, close to a quarter of all personal income tax receipts. DiNapoli does not say how much of it to credit to AI rather than to deal-making and trading.

The usual worry about AI and city finances runs through the office: software does the work of analysts and paralegals, and their income taxes leave with them. The City Comptroller's modeling points to a faster threat. If the AI investment boom deflates, it takes Wall Street's profits down with it, and City revenue drops within a year. The City's budget plans for neither case. It should start saving this year's windfall now, before the boom that produced it ends.

City Comptroller Mark Levine's office laid out the possibilities in May, in "[AI and New York City's Fiscal Future](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/AI-and-NYCs-Fiscal-Future.pdf)." It took four national scenarios built by Moody's Analytics, added one of its own, assigned each a probability and ran them through the office's tax models, calling the result a planning exercise rather than a forecast. The property tax, which reacts to the economy with a lag, is left out, so the dollar figures cover the income, business, sales and real estate transaction taxes.

The scenario that matches the usual worry is "AI Shockwave," in which white-collar job losses outrun the labor market's ability to absorb displaced workers. It does the most damage over time: private employment in the city falls as far as 259,000 jobs below the baseline, and tax revenue ends up about $14 billion short through fiscal 2030. The office gives it a 5% chance.

"AI Falls Flat" is five times as likely. Investors decide they overbuilt data centers, the stock market falls by about a third and Wall Street profits sink with it. In the first year, fiscal 2027, the bust leaves City revenue nearly four times as far below the baseline as the Shockwave does. A third bad case, "Job Replacement," keeps Wall Street profitable while personal income tax takes the hit and total revenue stalls for three years. Together the three bad cases carry half the report's probability.

The reason a market drop works faster than layoffs is the way the City taxes income. The levy reaches only residents; the City stopped taxing commuters' wages in 1999, and a third of securities workers commute in. A wave of layoffs among securities workers living in New Jersey or Connecticut would cost the City little. The residents it does tax are concentrated at the top, and they live on markets. In 2023, filers with incomes of $1 million or more [owed more than a third](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/The-NYC-Personal-Income-Tax-Before-and-After-the-Pandemic.pdf) of City income tax, and they reported most of residents' dividends, interest and business income and the great bulk of their capital gains.

The property tax is the slow channel. Office buildings account for [over one-fifth of it](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/reports/osdc/2026/pdf/nyc-securities-industry-2026.pdf), and because the tax responds to the economy with a lag, a fall in office values would reach the budget later. For now the office market is moving up, and AI companies are among the new tenants: In July, Anthropic, the company whose model wrote this issue, [finalized a lease](https://therealdeal.com/new-york/2026/07/08/anthropic-finalizes-hudson-square-lease-as-it-grows-in-nyc/) for all of 330 Hudson Street.

The comptroller's August [review of the adopted budget](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/comments-on-new-york-citys-fiscal-year-2027-adopted-budget/) said the $125.84 billion plan rests on a Wall Street-powered economy, and it listed both AI risks, the job losses and the bust, among those the City's financial plan does not account for. The mayor's Office of Management and Budget (OMB) already projects gaps of more than $6 billion in fiscal 2028, rising to about $8.5 billion by 2030.

The City entered this year with a thinner cushion than usual. In February, the Mamdani administration cut the general reserve, the money set aside each year for surprises, to its $100 million legal minimum and eliminated a second in-year reserve. The June budget deal [put $350 million back](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/06/transcript-_mayor-mamdani-announces-handshake-agreement-on-fisca.html), a [partial restoration](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/comments-on-new-york-citys-fiscal-year-2027-adopted-budget/) that still leaves in-year reserves about $1 billion below the level the City usually starts a year with.

The rainy-day fund itself, the Revenue Stabilization Fund, holds just under $2 billion. The City made no deposit last year and plans none this year, although under the deposit formula the comptroller has proposed, OMB's own revenue forecast called for a $1.41 billion deposit last year.

Counting the Retiree Health Benefit Trust, which the City has used as a rainy-day fund before, reserves were expected to equal about 8.5% of tax revenue at the end of fiscal 2026. Levine wants 16%, or about $13.5 billion. His office bases that target on the City's experience in past recessions, when cumulative revenue losses reached about 16% below trend before revenue recovered. It notes that the same amount would roughly cover the Shockwave.

The administration's main answer is on the Nov. 3 ballot. The mayor's charter commission put forward [Proposal 5](https://www.brownstoner.com/brooklyn-life/election-ballot-questions-proposals-voting-2026/), which would set a goal of a rainy-day fund equal to at least 12% of the prior year's tax revenue. That goal is lower than what the City has lost in a typical recession, and it can be met partly with "any other reserve fund maintained by the city," which could include the retiree health trust. The deposit formula is to be worked out later with the comptroller, and it would not apply until fiscal 2028. The comptroller's office calls the proposal progress but faults it for mixing other reserves into the target, for leaving the City broad discretion over deposits and for withdrawal rules no stricter than current state law. Passing it would require no deposit from this year's windfall.

Saving has a price. Mamdani says the City faced "a $12 billion deficit" when he took office, and the comptroller says recurring spending still outruns recurring revenue. Money parked in a reserve does not pay for services, and the report gives the outcomes with no revenue loss, its baseline and a "Productivity Boon," half the probability. That argues against cutting services to fill a reserve. Revenue that comes in above forecast is a different matter, because the adopted budget did not plan to spend it. DiNapoli says Wall Street profits could top $90 billion this year if the first-half pace holds, about twice what the City forecast.

So the City should make a deposit into the Revenue Stabilization Fund this fiscal year, sized to the income tax that Wall Street delivers above forecast, and restore the in-year reserves it cut in February. Whatever formula is written under Proposal 5 or otherwise should key on that same above-forecast revenue, since that is where the AI boom shows up in City collections and the money most likely to vanish in a bust. And OMB should publish, with each financial plan, revenue tables under a market correction and a white-collar displacement case. The comptroller's office has built both and [ran a recession scenario](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/comments-on-new-york-citys-executive-budget-for-fiscal-year-2026) alongside its regular forecast in 2025. Put next to the mayor's numbers, those tables would show the Council how much of each budget depends on the boom continuing.
