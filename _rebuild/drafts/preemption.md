On Oct. 5, the New York City Council held a [rare Committee of the Whole hearing](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/10/05/3278/) on the risks of artificial intelligence. Speaker Julie Menin asked each company representative to "quantify the risk posed by AI in the worst-case catastrophic scenario." Former researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind testified as whistleblowers. The hearing took up proposals for a whistleblower incentive program, a private right of action and third-party validation requirements. "When the federal government fails to act, cities take the lead," Menin said.

Ten months earlier, the Council had done something narrower. In November 2025 it [passed a package of bills](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2025/11/25/3012) its sponsors called the GUARD Act, which created an Office of Algorithmic Accountability to analyze the algorithmic tools City agencies use, required the office to set standards for how agencies develop, buy, deploy and use AI and required it to publish a list of the systems it has assessed. The lead bill [came back unsigned by Mayor Eric Adams](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6557537&GUID=14A351D1-8AED-448E-A8C0-355A39CB02BE) and became Local Law 188 of 2025 on Dec. 25.

The office was supposed to exist by June, and the City budget set aside $1.4 million for six employees. In July, [City & State reported](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) "there's no sign the office exists yet." At the Oct. 5 hearing, the Office of Technology and Innovation, which oversees the new office, [testified](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/media/Advocacy-Testimony-Intros-161-T20262602-T20262601-T20262606-T20262605-T20262599-T20262603-504-T20262600.pdf) that it "has been formally established" and that hiring for six positions in the fiscal 2027 budget was "actively underway." Menin told the agency's representative the office "is not up and running." As of Oct. 19, no director had been named.

Washington, the states and the industry are fighting over who regulates the companies that build AI. Since the Senate killed a 10-year moratorium on state and local AI laws in 2025, the main federal preemption proposals have left alone the rules a state writes for its own use of the technology. A city's rules for its own agencies are the nearest local equivalent, and they stand on firmer ground than any law the Council could aim at AI developers, a field Albany already regulates and Washington wants to preempt. New York City should work on those rules first, starting with the office it already created.

## The fight over the companies

In May 2025 the House [passed a budget bill](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr1eh/html/BILLS-119hr1eh.htm) with a 10-year moratorium: no "State or political subdivision thereof" could enforce any law "limiting, restricting, or otherwise regulating" AI models or automated decision systems. The text made no exception for a city's rules about its own agencies. On July 1, 2025, the Senate [voted 99 to 1](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1191/vote_119_1_00363.htm) to strike the provision; Sen. Thom Tillis cast the only no vote. Seventeen Republican governors and 40 state attorneys general had opposed it, according to [Sen. Maria Cantwell's committee staff](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/2025/7/senate-strikes-ai-moratorium-from-budget-reconciliation-bill-in-overwhelming-99-1-vote/).

The administration tried again by [executive order](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/16/2025-23092/ensuring-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence). Order 14365, signed Dec. 11, 2025, told the attorney general to set up an AI Litigation Task Force whose "sole responsibility" is to challenge state AI laws. It told the Commerce Department to list "onerous" state laws within 90 days and directed that states with such laws be made ineligible for some remaining federal broadband money. It singled out Colorado's law against "algorithmic discrimination." As of April, neither the Commerce list nor the broadband notice had been made public, [according to the law firm Proskauer](https://www.lawandtheworkplace.com/2026/04/what-president-trumps-ai-executive-order-14365-means-for-employers/).

The pressure has shown up in court instead. In April the Justice Department [moved to join a lawsuit](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-intervenes-xai-lawsuit-challenging-colorados-algorithmic-discrimination) that the AI company xAI had filed against the Colorado law, arguing it violates the equal protection clause. On April 27 a federal magistrate judge [approved an agreement](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-ai-law-in-flux-comprehensive-replacement-bill-signed-after-federal-court-blocks-predecessors-enforcement/) under which the state would hold off enforcing the law, and on May 14 Colorado [enacted a replacement](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189) that drops the detailed impact assessments and takes effect Jan. 1, 2027.

## What the federal proposals leave to the states

The executive order also told White House advisers to draft a preemption bill that would leave alone state laws on child safety, data center infrastructure and "State government procurement and use of AI." The White House's [March 20 legislative framework](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/03.20.26-National-Policy-Framework-for-Artificial-Intelligence-Legislative-Recommendations.pdf) kept that exception. Congress, it said, should not preempt "requirements governing a state's own use of AI, whether through procurement or services they provide like law enforcement and public education." The same page says, "States should not be permitted to regulate AI development." Hodan Omar of the Center for Data Innovation, part of a think tank whose funders include many of the largest technology firms, [told Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/03/what-to-watch-from-trumps-national-ai-standard) that a national framework should carve out "state procurement."

A bipartisan House discussion draft draws a similar line, less directly. The Great American AI Act, a 269-page text released June 4 by Reps. Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan, would [preempt state laws](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/04/bipartisan-ai-draft-proposes-three-year-preemption-of-state-laws/) "specifically regulating the development" of AI models for three years and would not apply to laws on use or deployment. As of mid-September, 11 bills carved from it had [cleared the House Science Committee](https://www.benton.org/headlines/11-ai-bills-cleared-committee-summer-none-has-reached-house-floor) and none had reached the floor. In July, Obernolte [told Punchbowl News](https://punchbowl.news/article/tech/obernolte-preemption-shift/) that changes to the draft could "absolutely" include a revamp of its state-law section.

Every one of these carve-outs says "state." None mentions cities, and Congress knows how to name them when it means to: the House moratorium covered any "political subdivision." Applying the carve-outs to New York City is an argument by analogy. A city's rules for its own agencies are the same kind of rule a state writes for its own, made by a subdivision of the state, but a future bill could protect one and not the other.

Rules for developers are where New York state has staked its claim. The Responsible AI Safety and Education Act, or RAISE Act, [signed Dec. 19, 2025](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-nation-leading-legislation-require-ai-frameworks-ai-frontier-models), and [rewritten by an amendment](https://www.wiley.law/alert-New-York-Finalizes-RAISE-Act-for-Frontier-AI-Models-Law-Takes-Effect-January-1-2027) Gov. Kathy Hochul signed March 27, covers "large frontier developers" with more than $500 million in annual revenue. Starting Jan. 1, 2027, they must publish safety protocols and report critical safety incidents within 72 hours to a new office in the state Department of Financial Services. Hochul's office said the law "builds on" California's framework, Senate Bill 53, which Gov. Gavin Newsom [signed in September 2025](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2025/09/29/governor-newsom-signs-sb-53-advancing-californias-world-leading-artificial-intelligence-industry/). A summary from Trahan's office says frontier safety laws in California, New York and Illinois would be "federalized" under the House draft. A city law aimed at the same developers would add a second set of rules on top of the state's.

## The constitutional objection

Kevin Frazier, the AI Innovation and Law Fellow at the University of Texas School of Law, has made the case at length against state AI rules that reach beyond a state's borders. Writing in [The Regulatory Review](https://www.theregreview.org/2025/08/25/frazier-extraterritorial-limits-on-states-as-laboratories-of-ai-policy/) in August 2025, he argued that the Constitution bars states from projecting their laws onto out-of-state companies and that frontier AI makes such projection unavoidable. "Labs cannot train models specific to the policy requirements of each state, a handful of states, or even a single state," he wrote.

The point applies with more force to the bills aired on Oct. 5. If a state law aimed at frontier labs reaches past the state line, a city law reaches further, and every city that writes one adds another set of requirements for the same models.

His argument has less to say about a city deciding how its own agencies may use a screening model or a chatbot. The Supreme Court drew a related line in 1983, when Boston required that at least half the workers on city-funded construction be residents. To the extent the city spent "only its own funds," [the Court held](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/460/204), it "was a market participant," and the commerce clause did not stop it. That doctrine would not bar Congress from preempting a city if it chose to.

## What the City can do this year

The developer bills aired on Oct. 5 would spend the City's time on the ground most likely to be preempted. The rules for its own agencies are already law, and the office that enforces them is still hiring.

Local Law 188 [has the tools](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6557537&GUID=14A351D1-8AED-448E-A8C0-355A39CB02BE). The mayor appoints a director of algorithmic accountability. The office runs pre-deployment risk assessments, takes public comments on specific tools and sets up a complaint process with the Department of Investigation. The law also amends the annual algorithmic tools report: for each tool, agencies must now say whether the office has assessed it, summarize the result and note any corrective action, with the compilation due each March 31. [Local Law 193](https://intro.nyc/0926-2024) requires the office to set standards that every agency must meet when it develops, buys, deploys or uses "public-impacting" AI.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani should name the director this fall and fill the six funded positions. The office's first assignment should be the tools agencies already report, so that the March 31, 2027, report carries an assessment status for each one.

The Council's next AI hearing should then be an oversight hearing on that report, held in April. Members should go down the list tool by tool and ask whether the office has assessed each one.
