On Oct. 5, the New York City Council held a [rare Committee of the Whole hearing](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/10/05/3278/) on the risks of artificial intelligence. Speaker Julie Menin asked each company representative to "quantify the risk posed by AI in the worst-case catastrophic scenario," and former researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind testified as whistleblowers. On the table were proposals for a whistleblower incentive program, a private right of action and third-party validation requirements. "When the federal government fails to act, cities take the lead," Menin said.

At the same hearing she turned to the City's own house. Menin cited press reports that Jiahao Chen, who had run AI and machine learning work at the Office of Technology and Innovation for two and a half years, had resigned in frustration over positions the Office of Management and Budget was holding up. She tied his exit to the Office of Algorithmic Accountability, which the Council created last year and which "was supposed to be up and running by the city in June. But it is not up and running."

The office came out of a package the Council passed in November 2025, the [Guaranteeing Unbiased AI Regulation and Disclosure, or GUARD, Act](https://www.cityandstateny.com/politics/2025/11/new-york-city-will-try-again-regulate-ai/409803/). The bills [charged the office](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2025/11/25/3012) with reviewing the algorithmic tools City agencies use and setting standards for how they build, buy and deploy AI. The lead bill [came back unsigned by Mayor Eric Adams](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6557537&GUID=14A351D1-8AED-448E-A8C0-355A39CB02BE) and became Local Law 188 on Dec. 25. The City budget set aside $1.4 million for six employees, and in July [City & State found](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) "no sign the office exists yet." At the hearing, the technology office [testified](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/media/Advocacy-Testimony-Intros-161-T20262602-T20262601-T20262606-T20262605-T20262599-T20262603-504-T20262600.pdf) that the office had been "formally established" and that hiring was underway. No director had been named by Oct. 19.

The Council is aiming its new bills at the companies that build AI, the one field where Washington is fighting hardest to shut out states and cities and where Albany has already legislated. The office governs something else: how City agencies use the technology. That is the kind of rule the main federal preemption proposals have left to the states. The Council should set the developer bills aside and spend its leverage getting the office built.

## What Washington wants to preempt

In May 2025 the House [passed a budget bill](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr1eh/html/BILLS-119hr1eh.htm) that would have barred any "State or political subdivision thereof" from enforcing laws regulating AI models or automated decision systems for 10 years, with no exception for a city's rules about its own agencies. Republican governors and state attorneys general objected, [according to Sen. Maria Cantwell's committee staff](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/2025/7/senate-strikes-ai-moratorium-from-budget-reconciliation-bill-in-overwhelming-99-1-vote/), and on July 1 the Senate [voted 99 to 1](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1191/vote_119_1_00363.htm) to strip it.

The administration tried again with an [executive order](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/16/2025-23092/ensuring-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence) in December. It set up a Justice Department task force whose "sole responsibility" is to challenge state AI laws, told the Commerce Department to list "onerous" ones and directed that states with such laws lose some remaining federal broadband money. It singled out Colorado's law against "algorithmic discrimination." The Commerce list still had not been made public in April, [the law firm Proskauer noted](https://www.lawandtheworkplace.com/2026/04/what-president-trumps-ai-executive-order-14365-means-for-employers/), so the pressure came through the courts. The Justice Department [moved to join](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-intervenes-xai-lawsuit-challenging-colorados-algorithmic-discrimination) a lawsuit the AI company xAI had filed against the Colorado law. A federal magistrate judge [approved an agreement](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-ai-law-in-flux-comprehensive-replacement-bill-signed-after-federal-court-blocks-predecessors-enforcement/) under which the state would hold off enforcing it, and in May Colorado [enacted a replacement](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189) without the detailed impact assessments.

## The exception written for states

The same order told White House advisers to draft a preemption bill that would leave alone state laws on "State government procurement and use of AI." The White House [legislative framework](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/03.20.26-National-Policy-Framework-for-Artificial-Intelligence-Legislative-Recommendations.pdf) released in March kept the line. Congress should not preempt "requirements governing a state's own use of AI, whether through procurement or services they provide like law enforcement and public education," it said, while "States should not be permitted to regulate AI development." Hodan Omar of the Center for Data Innovation, part of a think tank whose funders include many of the largest technology firms, [told Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/03/what-to-watch-from-trumps-national-ai-standard) that a national framework should carve out "state procurement."

A bipartisan House discussion draft, the Great American AI Act from Reps. Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan, gets there less directly. It would [preempt state laws](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/04/bipartisan-ai-draft-proposes-three-year-preemption-of-state-laws/) "specifically regulating the development" of AI models and leave laws on use or deployment alone. Pieces of it [cleared the House Science Committee](https://www.benton.org/headlines/11-ai-bills-cleared-committee-summer-none-has-reached-house-floor) this summer without reaching the floor, and Obernolte [told Punchbowl News](https://punchbowl.news/article/tech/obernolte-preemption-shift/) the state-law section could "absolutely" be revamped.

Every one of these carve-outs says "state." Congress names cities when it means to, as the House moratorium did with "political subdivision." A city's rules for its own agencies are the same kind of rule, made by a subdivision of the state, but a future bill could protect one and leave the other exposed. New York's House delegation should push to write "political subdivision" into whatever carve-out survives.

Rules for developers are where the fight is, and Albany already holds that ground. The Responsible AI Safety and Education Act, or RAISE Act, [signed](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-nation-leading-legislation-require-ai-frameworks-ai-frontier-models) by Gov. Kathy Hochul in December 2025 and [rewritten](https://www.wiley.law/alert-New-York-Finalizes-RAISE-Act-for-Frontier-AI-Models-Law-Takes-Effect-January-1-2027) in March, requires developers with more than $500 million in annual revenue to publish safety protocols and report critical incidents to a new state office starting in 2027. Hochul's office said the law "builds on" California's Senate Bill 53, which Gov. Gavin Newsom [signed in September 2025](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2025/09/29/governor-newsom-signs-sb-53-advancing-californias-world-leading-artificial-intelligence-industry/). A summary from Trahan's office says those state frontier laws would be "federalized" under the House draft.

A City law aimed at the same labs would sit on top of the state's and would invite the argument Kevin Frazier, the AI Innovation and Law Fellow at the University of Texas School of Law, has made against states [in The Regulatory Review](https://www.theregreview.org/2025/08/25/frazier-extraterritorial-limits-on-states-as-laboratories-of-ai-policy/): the Constitution bars them from projecting their laws onto out-of-state companies, and frontier AI makes that projection unavoidable. "Labs cannot train models specific to the policy requirements of each state, a handful of states, or even a single state," he wrote.

His argument does not reach a city deciding which screening model or chatbot its own agencies may use. When Boston required that at least half the workers on city-funded construction be residents, the Supreme Court [held](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/460/204) in 1983 that to the extent the city spent "only its own funds," it "was a market participant," and the commerce clause did not stop it. That doctrine shields a city from a lawsuit, not from Congress, which is why the wording of a federal carve-out matters.

## Build the office first

Local Law 188 [has the tools](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6557537&GUID=14A351D1-8AED-448E-A8C0-355A39CB02BE). The mayor appoints a director of algorithmic accountability. The office assesses tools before agencies deploy them, takes public comment on specific tools and sets up a complaint process with the Department of Investigation. Each March 31, the City's annual report on agency algorithmic tools must now say whether the office has assessed each tool, what it found and what was corrected. [Local Law 193](https://intro.nyc/0926-2024) requires the office to set standards that every agency must meet when it develops, buys or uses "public-impacting" AI.

None of it works without people. Mayor Zohran Mamdani should name the director this fall and fill the six funded positions, and if the budget office is sitting on them, City Hall should tell it to stop. The office's first assignment should be the tools agencies already report, so that next March's report carries an assessment for every one.

The Council has its own job. In place of bills aimed at AI labs, which Albany already regulates and Washington is trying to preempt, it should hold an oversight hearing on that report in April and go down the list tool by tool, asking which ones the office has assessed and what it found.
