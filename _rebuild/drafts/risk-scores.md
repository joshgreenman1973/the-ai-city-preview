Starting in 2012, getting arrested once in Chicago was enough to earn a number. The Chicago Police Department ran a series of models meant to predict who would shoot or be shot, and [according to the city's inspector general](https://igchicago.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/OIG-Advisory-Concerning-CPDs-Predictive-Risk-Models-.pdf), everyone arrested at least once in the prior four years got a score, "regardless of whether they had a history of violence." By the last version of the model, nearly 400,000 people had one.

Any sworn officer could look the scores up. The department wrote them into arrest reports, shared them with the county sheriff and sent them to prosecutors. The inspector general found that the scores had gone nearly two and a half years without an update, and that people who got a police warning because of them could face harsher charging on later arrests, convicted or not. Chicago shut the program down in November 2019.

In Los Angeles, a [review by the Police Commission's inspector general](https://www.oig.lacity.org/_files/ugd/b2dd23_21f6fe20f1b84c179abf440d4c049219.pdf) found that 44% of the people the Los Angeles Police Department had labeled "Chronic Offenders," among those with detailed point tallies, had zero or one arrest for a violent crime. The department proposed telling people about the label and letting them appeal it. Instead it [ended the program, Operation LASER (for Los Angeles Strategic Extraction and Restoration)](https://www.latimes.com/local/lanow/la-me-laser-lapd-crime-data-program-20190412-story.html), in April 2019, and a year later, citing the pandemic budget, [dropped PredPol](https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2020-04-21/lapd-ends-predictive-policing-program), its software for forecasting property crime.

Chicago's inspector general listed the agencies that could see the scores. The people being scored were not among them. A score is defensible when the person it describes, that person's lawyer and the public can see how it works and check it. New York City runs a scoring tool that meets that standard, and in violent felony cases judges set its advice aside most of the time, more often for Black defendants than for white ones. The City pays for the score. It should publish, every year, how often judges depart from it and for whom.

## The score New York City uses

The New York Police Department lists no tool in the City's [algorithm inventory](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/City-Government/Algorithmic-Tools-Compliance-Report/jaw4-yuem) that scores individuals' risk. The score New York City does use on people is calculated before arraignment. Staff from the [New York City Criminal Justice Agency](https://www.nycja.org/our-work/release-assessment) (CJA), a nonprofit under contract with the Mayor's Office of Criminal Justice, interview nearly everyone arrested and held, then run a [release assessment](https://www.nycja.org/assets/downloads/CJA-Brief-46_updated-release-assessment.pdf) that predicts whether the person will come back to court. The current version, built by the firm Luminosity and the University of Chicago Crime Lab from a file of 1.6 million arrests, went live in November 2019. It docks points for past bench warrants, recent convictions, pending cases, living at an address less than three years and having no phone where the person can be reached.

[State law](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CPL/510.10) ties release decisions to whether a person is likely to return to court, not to danger to the public, so that is all the tool predicts. The point values are published, CJA posts an interactive version online, and the judge, the prosecutor and the defense lawyer [all receive the same report](https://datacollaborativeforjustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Release_Assessment_Report.pdf), which shows how the score was reached.

The tool has held up. A [2025 revalidation](https://www.nycja.org/assets/downloads/Release-Assessment-Validation-1.0-20250512-2.pdf) on cases from 2022 and 2023 found it sorted people more accurately than it had on the 2014 cases used to build it. Of released people it had recommended for release on their own recognizance, 10% drew a bench warrant before their case ended. It recommended release for Black, Hispanic and white people at close to the same rates, slightly less often for white people. Researchers at the Data Collaborative for Justice suggest one possible reason it avoids the racial skew found in other tools: those predict rearrest, and this one predicts showing up.

Researchers disagree about whether scores like this improve on judges. Using New York City arrests from 2008 to 2013, Jon Kleinberg, Jens Ludwig, Sendhil Mullainathan and colleagues [simulated](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/133/1/237/4095198) an algorithm that could cut failures to appear in court by up to 24.7% with no more people jailed, or cut jailing by 41.9% with no rise in failures to appear, while reducing racial disparities. That was a simulation. When a risk tool was adopted in sentencing, [Megan Stevenson and Jennifer Doleac found](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20220620) that judges departed from its recommendations in systematic ways, and the tool produced no detectable gain in public safety or drop in incarceration.

The fight over fairness began with ProPublica's 2016 analysis of a commercial tool called [COMPAS](https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing) in Broward County, Fla. Its mistakes ran in opposite directions by race: Black defendants who did not reoffend were labeled higher risk nearly twice as often as white defendants who did not, while white defendants who did reoffend were more often labeled low risk. The maker, Northpointe, [answered](https://www.propublica.org/article/bias-in-criminal-risk-scores-is-mathematically-inevitable-researchers-say) that its scores were equally accurate for both groups. Both were true, and [Alexandra Chouldechova](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00056) and, separately, [Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Manish Raghavan](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807) showed why: when the predicted outcome occurs at different rates in two groups, no score short of a perfect one can meet every common definition of fairness at once.

So every risk tool builds in a choice about whose errors to equalize. Northpointe stated its choice in public but kept its formula private, and in State v. Loomis, Wisconsin's Supreme Court [let a judge use a COMPAS score at sentencing](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-130/state-v-loomis/) even though the method had been disclosed to neither the court nor the defendant.

## When judges set the score aside

New York's 2020 [bail law](https://www.nycja.org/assets/downloads/CJA-Brief-46_updated-release-assessment.pdf) ended bail for most misdemeanor and nonviolent felony cases, but nearly every violent felony charge is still eligible. A [Data Collaborative for Justice study](https://datacollaborativeforjustice.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Release_Assessment_Report.pdf) of city arraignments from 2021 to 2023 found the tool recommended release on recognizance for 79% of people charged with violent felonies. Judges granted it to 25%.

Race made a difference in those cases. Where the tool recommended release, judges released 43% of white defendants and 26% of Black defendants. They also set bail or ordered jail without bail for Black defendants 15 percentage points more often than for white defendants.

Some of that gap comes from differences among the cases. Controlling for other factors shrinks the Black-white gap in release to 9 points. The statute also lets judges weigh things the tool leaves out, including firearm history and whether a charge involves serious harm, and the authors call full agreement with the tool "unrealistic and unnecessary." But they trace the disparity that remains to judges giving extra weight to criminal history and unstable housing, which the score already counts. And they cannot tell whether judges looked at the score at all.

The law already requires judges to explain the basis for a release decision on the record or in writing. The most recent published count of those decisions, set against the score, covers 2021 to 2023, and researchers got the data because CJA agreed to share it. The study's authors put the responsibility for informing judges on the City agencies that created the assessment and pay to run it. The Mayor's Office of Criminal Justice, which holds the CJA contract, can make publication a term of that contract: each year, how often judges follow a release recommendation and how often they set bail or remand instead, by borough, charge and race. The state Office of Court Administration could log a reason each time a judge departs, so the count can be read against the explanations the law already demands. If firearm history or serious harm the score cannot see explains the gap, the reasons will show it. If the explanation is the same criminal history and housing the score already weighed, the City will know its tool is being overruled for reasons it already took into account.
