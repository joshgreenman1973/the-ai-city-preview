Since March 2024, staff at the Mayor's Office to End Domestic and Gender-Based Violence have used ChatGPT to draft emails to the public and to write questions for job interviews. Three more offices under the mayor took it up in 2025, one of them to tighten text, some of which goes to the public. The City knows this because agencies report their algorithmic tools each year under Local Law 35, and the [2025 list](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=1000) says these offices put personal identifying information into the tool and get it back in the answers. It does not say whose accounts they log in with or what the vendor may do with what it receives.

Those are the uses agencies chose to report. [Gallup found](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/702983/adoption-rapidly-growing-public-sector.aspx) that 43% of U.S. public-sector employees used artificial intelligence (AI) at work at least a few times a year by late 2025, up from 17% in mid-2023, and it credits much of the growth to workers trying the tools on their own. A [Thomson Reuters Institute article](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/government/shadow-ai-unsanctioned-tools/), drawing on the company's own survey, put the share of government professionals using tools their employer never approved at about 27%. No one has surveyed New York City's workforce.

City workers will keep using these tools. City Hall can decide whether they do it on accounts the City pays for and can audit or on personal logins it never sees. New York City should buy or build a logged AI assistant for every employee who completes training, move the mayor's own offices onto it first and write the rules for its use into the next contract with District Council 37 (DC 37). The union's current deal covers nearly 90,000 City workers and expires Nov. 6, 2026. State law already bars using AI to cut those workers' jobs, which leaves the union much less to fear from the bargain.

## Rules without accounts

The Office of Technology and Innovation (OTI) has written the rule already. Its [generative AI guidance](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/New-York-City-Generative-AI-Use-Guidance.pdf), revised Dec. 30, 2025, says citywide cybersecurity policy forbids personal accounts, requires City-managed accounts for all such work and tells agencies to keep logs that can be audited. OTI's [first version, from March 2024](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/about/preliminary-use-guidance-general-artificial-intelligence.pdf) warned that prompts may be public records under the state Freedom of Information Law.

The City's own tool list shows pilots instead. OTI tested Microsoft Copilot with a small group of its own staff in the summer of 2025, on work that included analyzing proposed legislation, and the Department of Environmental Protection is trying it in its public affairs office. OTI has published nothing about how its pilot went, and the list describes no assistant for the workforce as a whole.

New Jersey shows what the fix can cost. The state [built its own assistant](https://innovation.nj.gov/projects/ai-assistant) in-house, runs it on state infrastructure and says about 20,000 employees have used it, at roughly $1 per user per month against about $20 for a commercial license. Even at the commercial price, that is a modest bill for knowing where the City's data goes.

## What the state law settles

DC 37's answer is to keep the tools out. ["For many reasons, AI has no place in the City's workplaces,"](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?M=F&ID=14332995&GUID=FDDEE48F-B8F5-4A36-BEB0-5C2DBAC79AEB) Rose Lovaglio-Miller, a DC 37 associate director, told the Council in June 2025, and she called a proposed study of AI's effect on City operations a hunt for "a technocratic justification for austerity." A [rank-and-file petition](https://actionnetwork.org/petitions/dc37-contract-petition) asks the union's bargaining committee to limit AI at work. Keeping AI out on paper would leave the mayor's offices doing what they do now, on accounts the City's list does not describe.

Albany has since taken most of the jobs fight off the table. The state's Legislative Oversight of Automated Decision-making in Government (LOADinG) Act [began in 2024](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S07543&term=2023&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y) as a protection for state workers. A [December 2025 law](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S07599&term=2025&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y) extended it to local governments, and a [follow-up signed Feb. 13, 2026](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S08831&term=2025&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y) wrote city employees into its job protections by name. AI may not be used to fire or displace them, to cut their hours, pay or benefits or to take over their duties. Those protections [run until July 1, 2028](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S00822&term=2025&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y), and they apply only to systems that "directly impact the public." A [Manhattan Institute brief](https://manhattan.institute/article/new-yorks-loading-act-protecting-public-sector-jobs-from-ai) noted in September that a narrow reading could leave a drafting assistant outside it. No court or agency has ruled on that, and nothing in the law stands in the way of handing a caseworker a tool.

A log of every prompt is something a manager could mine for discipline, which is why OTI's guidance tells agencies to ask whether a new tool raises concerns about workplace surveillance. It is also the best evidence a worker could have. Lovaglio-Miller told the Council that members "will suffer disciplinary consequences for mistakes that are no fault of their own," and that this is already happening "with the little automation currently in use." When a chatbot's error ends up in a notice to the public, the log shows what the worker asked and what the machine answered. DC 37 should want that record to exist and should bargain over who gets to read it.

## What the contract should say

A City Hall spokesperson said in July that the administration would use AI ["only where it demonstrably improves life for New Yorkers"](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) and would not allow it "at the expense of workers." The contract is the place to hold it to that. Pennsylvania offers a model. Gov. Josh Shapiro [put in writing](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/governor/documents/2025.3.21%20gov%20shapiro%20letter%20on%20ai%20and%20employees.pdf) that AI "will not be used to replace human supervision" of employee performance, and the state set up a [joint AI group](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/oa/newsroom/shapiro-admin-expands-safe-responsible-ai-improve-services.html) with Service Employees International Union (SEIU) Local 668 before widening access to more than 3,000 workers, all trained first. The payoff there still rests on the workers' word. Pilot participants [reported saving an average of 95 minutes](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/oa/newsroom/icymi--shapiro-administration-s-generative-ai-pilot-for-state-wo.html) on the days they used ChatGPT, in a program Carnegie Mellon University and OpenAI helped shape, and the figure is their own estimate.

A side letter to the DC 37 deal should give a City-managed account to any employee who finishes training, so the ban on personal accounts has something behind it. Albany, which [began offering its own Gemini-based assistant](https://governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-delivers-artificial-intelligence-training-tool-new-york-state-workforce) to state agencies in April, [recommended in June](https://its.ny.gov/generative-artificial-intelligence-use-workplace) that state agencies log who uses such tools, what they put in and how the answers are used, and keep the logs at least 90 days. Compliance is voluntary there. The City should make the same terms binding. Managers should be barred from using an individual's log to rate or discipline that worker, except when restricted data is misused, and the union should get to see the log whenever a member is disciplined over an AI-assisted error. OTI should publish counts of users and uses by agency every quarter.

The Council has already ordered the study that would show whether any of this pays off. [Local Law 25 of 2026](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6874682&GUID=0B6A4E8B-4D49-437B-BB70-50B898D2BD7F), passed in December and law since January without a mayor's signature, directs the City's technology department to study how algorithmic tools affect City employees, including what the employees themselves report, and to deliver a report three months after the study ends. It sets no date for the study to end. The side letter should set one: a finished report, built on the first year of logs and quarterly counts, before the state's job protections lapse in July 2028. If the tools save City workers time, that report is where the City proves it. If they don't, the union will have the evidence in hand when Albany decides whether to extend the protections.
