Since March 2024, staff at the Mayor's Office to End Domestic and Gender-Based Violence have used ChatGPT to draft public emails and to write job interview questions. Three more offices under New York City's mayor took it up in 2025. The City's [2025 algorithmic tools filing](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/jaw4-yuem.json?$limit=1000), required by Local Law 35, lists the Mayor's Office use as daily. The Office of Talent and Workforce Development, one of the 2025 arrivals, uses it monthly to tighten text, some of which goes to the public. The filing marks identifying information, as City law defines the term, in both what goes into the tool and what comes out. It calls the product "off-the-shelf."

Those are the uses agencies chose to report. Nationally, [Gallup found](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/702983/adoption-rapidly-growing-public-sector.aspx) that 43% of public-sector employees used artificial intelligence (AI) at work at least a few times a year in late 2025, up from 17% in mid-2023. Only 37% said their employer had a clear AI strategy. Gallup attributes some of the rise to a low barrier to entry, which means "employees can experiment with AI on their own." A [Thomson Reuters Institute article](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/government/shadow-ai-unsanctioned-tools/) citing the company's own survey put the share of government professionals using AI tools their employer has not sanctioned at about 27%. It is one company's survey, and it does not measure New York City's workforce.

City workers will keep using these tools. City Hall can decide whether that use runs through accounts the City controls and records, under rules its unions helped write, or through personal logins nobody sees. New York City should issue a sanctioned, logged tool and settle the rules in its next contract with District Council 37 (DC 37), whose current deal covers nearly 90,000 City workers and expires Nov. 6, 2026. The state's AI job protections, which now extend to City employees, make that deal easier to strike.

## What the City already requires

The Office of Technology and Innovation's (OTI) [updated generative AI guidance](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/New-York-City-Generative-AI-Use-Guidance.pdf), dated Dec. 30, 2025, says "using personal accounts is prohibited by citywide cybersecurity policies." It tells employees to "use pre-approved City-managed accounts for all GenAI-related work" and agencies to make sure "proper logging and audit capabilities are in place." OTI's [first version, from March 2024](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/oti/downloads/pdf/about/preliminary-use-guidance-general-artificial-intelligence.pdf), warned that prompts "may be covered" by the state Freedom of Information Law.

That guidance assumes the City has accounts to hand out. The Local Law 35 filing shows pilots and office-by-office use instead. OTI tested Microsoft Copilot with "select staff" from June to August 2025, on tasks that included "legal analysis of proposed legislation." The Department of Environmental Protection is piloting Copilot in its public affairs shop. No results from the OTI pilot have been made public, and the filing describes nothing like a tool for the whole workforce.

## What the state law does

The state's Legislative Oversight of Automated Decision-making in Government (LOADinG) Act has changed shape three times, and the current version reaches City workers. The [original, signed in December 2024](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S07543&term=2023&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y), covered state agencies only. A [February 2025 chapter amendment](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S00822&term=2025&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y) dropped its impact assessments and moved the job protections into the Civil Service Law. A [December 2025 law](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S07599&term=2025&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y) extended the act to cities and other local governments. Its own [chapter amendment, signed Feb. 13, 2026](https://nyassembly.gov/leg/?default_fld=&leg_video=&bn=S08831&term=2025&Summary=Y&Actions=Y&Text=Y), stripped out the local impact assessments but kept the job protections and wrote city employees into them by name.

As it stands, the use of AI systems "shall not result in the discharge, displacement or loss of position, including partial displacement such as a reduction in the hours of non-overtime work, wages, or employment benefits." Nor may duties now performed by city employees be transferred "to an artificial intelligence system." The protections expire July 1, 2028. The [Civil Service Employees Association](https://cseany.org/?p=20216) (CSEA) read the December law the same way: its protections "will now extend to employees of any county, city, town, village."

The law limits what AI may replace and says nothing against giving a worker a tool. Its protections also attach only to systems that may "directly impact the public," a definition written around activities that affect the public's safety, welfare or rights. A [Manhattan Institute brief](https://manhattan.institute/article/new-yorks-loading-act-protecting-public-sector-jobs-from-ai) noted in September that a narrow reading could leave a drafting assistant outside that definition, and no court or agency has ruled on the question. Either way, a logged chatbot on a caseworker's screen and a statutory ban on using AI to eliminate that caseworker's job can coexist.

## The case against

DC 37 objects to the premise. ["For many reasons, AI has no place in the City's workplaces,"](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?M=F&ID=14332995&GUID=FDDEE48F-B8F5-4A36-BEB0-5C2DBAC79AEB) a DC 37 associate director, Rose Lovaglio-Miller, told the Council in June 2025. She called a proposed study of AI's effect on the civil service "an exploratory committee to find a technocratic justification for austerity." A [rank-and-file petition](https://actionnetwork.org/petitions/dc37-contract-petition) aimed at the union's bargaining committee asks to "limit use of AI in our workplaces and set standards for its use."

Some of the worry is warranted. The productivity evidence is thin and self-reported: Pennsylvania's pilot participants [said they saved an average of 95 minutes](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/oa/newsroom/icymi--shapiro-administration-s-generative-ai-pilot-for-state-wo.html) on the days they used ChatGPT, and the state's release describes no outside measurement. The state protections expire in 2028. A log of every prompt is a record a manager could mine. OTI's guidance itself asks agencies whether a new tool "could raise concerns about workplace surveillance."

Keeping AI out on paper would leave the mayor's offices using ChatGPT as they do now, on accounts whose terms the City's filing does not describe. DC 37's testimony also contains an argument for a record. Its members, Lovaglio-Miller said, "will suffer disciplinary consequences for mistakes that are no fault of their own," and "we are already experiencing this with the little automation currently in use." When a chatbot's error lands in a notice to the public, a log showing what the worker asked and what the machine returned is evidence on the worker's side.

## Governments that went first

Pennsylvania ran a pilot with 175 employees at 14 agencies, and Gov. Josh Shapiro then [put eight principles in writing](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/governor/documents/2025.3.21%20gov%20shapiro%20letter%20on%20ai%20and%20employees.pdf), including that AI "will not be used to replace human supervision" where employee performance is judged. The state formed a [labor-management AI group](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/oa/newsroom/shapiro-admin-expands-safe-responsible-ai-improve-services.html) with Service Employees International Union (SEIU) Local 668 and by April 2026 had more than 3,000 employees on generative AI tools, with training required first. New Jersey [built its own assistant](https://innovation.nj.gov/projects/ai-assistant), hosted on state infrastructure; about 20,000 state employees have used it, at roughly $1 per user per month by the state's count.

New York State's rollout bears most directly on the City, since the same job protections and Freedom of Information Law apply to both workforces. In April 2026 Gov. Kathy Hochul [took AI Pro](https://governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-delivers-artificial-intelligence-training-tool-new-york-state-workforce), a state-built assistant running on Google's Gemini, past a pilot with more than 1,200 users, offering it to more than 50 state agencies and a workforce of more than 100,000. Two months later the state's technology office [issued a guideline](https://its.ny.gov/generative-artificial-intelligence-use-workplace) saying all interactions with approved tools "should be logged for a minimum of 90 days," tracking "who uses GenAI systems, what inputs are provided to the system, and how outputs are utilized." Compliance, the guideline adds, is "not compulsory." A TechPolicy.Press commentary [has faulted](https://www.techpolicy.press/state-ai-rollouts-are-outrunning-their-own-governance) the state for calling AI Pro "a secure environment" without publishing what that means.

## A clause for the next contract

A City Hall spokesperson said in July that the administration will use AI ["only where it demonstrably improves life for New Yorkers"](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/07/mamdanis-government-has-started-embrace-ai-will-he/414872/) and will not allow it "at the expense of workers." The DC 37 talks are a place to write that down. The union's [2023 deal](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2023/02/mayor-adams-dc-37-tentative-contract-agreement-providing-fair-wage-increases-and) created a committee on flexible work, and its successor can carry an AI side letter with four terms.

First, the City provides a City-managed generative AI account to any employee who completes training, so the guidance's ban on personal accounts has something behind it. Second, prompts and outputs are kept for at least 90 days, the minimum in the state's guideline, for security and public-records purposes. Third, individual logs may not be used to evaluate or discipline a worker, except in cases of misuse of restricted data, and the union gets the right to see them when a member is disciplined over an AI-assisted error. Fourth, OTI publishes counts of users and uses by agency every quarter.

The Council has already ordered a study of what these tools do to City jobs. It passed [Local Law 25 of 2026](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6874682&GUID=0B6A4E8B-4D49-437B-BB70-50B898D2BD7F) on Dec. 18, 2025, and the bill became law on Jan. 17, 2026, without a signature, after a 30-day window for mayoral action that opened under Mayor Eric Adams and closed under Mayor Zohran Mamdani. The law directs the City's technology department to study how algorithmic tools affect City employees, including their feedback, and to report within three months of finishing. It sets no deadline for the study itself. The side letter could set one: a finished report, drawing on the first year of logs and quarterly counts, before the state's job protections expire on July 1, 2028.
